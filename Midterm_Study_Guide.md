### 1.2 The Three Meanings of "Classical" & Western Music Eras (PPT 1, Slide 4)
1. **Three Definitions of "Classical"**:
   * **Classics**: Any art/work of enduring excellence and timeless value across generations.
   * **Classical Music (broad genre)**: Western art music tradition from the Middle Ages to the present (as opposed to pop, jazz, or folk music).
   * **Classical Period (specific historical era)**: The era between **1750 and 1820** (Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven).
2. **Master Timeline of Western Music Periods**:
   * **Ancient Greek Music**: Antiquity (Pythagoras c. 580–500 BC, Plato, Aristotle)
   * **Medieval Period**: **c. 1000–1400** *(Gregorian chants originate earlier in the 6th–10th centuries)*
   * **Renaissance Period**: **c. 1400–1600**
   * **Common Practice Period** *(the era governed by the Major/Minor **tonal system**)*:
     * **Baroque Period**: **1600–1750** *(1750 = death of J. S. Bach)*
     * **Classical Period**: **1750–1820** *(1820s = late Beethoven; Beethoven dies in 1827)*
     * **Romantic Period**: **c. 1800–1910** *(overlaps with late Classical)*
   * **20th (Modern) & 21st (Contemporary) Century**: **c. 1890–present**

#### Aria vs. Recitativo (Recitative) — PPT 1, Slide 18
* **Recitativo (Recitative)**: **"Telling a story musically"** — speech-like singing that advances the plot/dialogue rapidly, with minimal, sparse accompaniment (basso continuo) and flexible rhythm following natural speech.
* **Aria**: **Emotional reflection / lyrical song** — the plot freezes while the character expresses deep emotion (*affection*) in a structured, melodic, virtuosic song accompanied by the full orchestra.

### 1.5 Italian Tempo & Dynamics Terms (PPT 1, Slide 26)
#### Tempo Marks (Slowest $\rightarrow$ Fastest)
1. **Lento / Largo**: Very slow, broad, stately (*e.g., Handel's "Largo" from Xerxes*)
2. **Adagio**: Slow, leisurely, expressive (*at ease*)
3. **Andante**: Moderately slow, at a **"walking pace"** (*e.g., Haydn's Surprise & Clock 2nd mvts*)
4. **Moderato**: Moderate speed
5. **Allegro**: Fast, bright, cheerful (standard 1st & 4th movement tempo)
6. **Vivace**: Lively, brisk, faster than Allegro
7. **Presto**: Very fast (*Prestissimo* = as fast as possible)

#### Dynamics (Volume)
* **$p$ (piano)** = soft | **$mp$ (mezzo-piano)** = moderately soft | **$pp$ (pianissimo)** = very soft | **$ppp$** = extremely soft
* **$f$ (forte)** = loud | **$mf$ (mezzo-forte)** = moderately loud | **$ff$ (fortissimo)** = very loud | **$fff$** = extremely loud
* **$sfz$ (sforzando)** = sudden, forceful accent on a single note/chord
* **Crescendo ($<$)** = gradually getting louder | **Decrescendo / Diminuendo ($>$)** = gradually getting softer
* **Terraced Dynamics (Baroque hallmark!)**: Abrupt, step-like shifts between loud ($f$) and soft ($p$) volumes (achieved by adding/subtracting instruments in a concerto grosso or switching harpsichord/organ manuals), rather than gradual crescendos.

#### Slide 4 (p. 01) — Three Meanings of 'Classical' & The Chronological Timeline of Western Music (Introduction & Chronology)
- **On the Slide**: `Definition: 1. classics; 2. classical music; 3. classical period | Periods: Ancient Greek music | Medieval: circa 1000–1400 | Renaissance: c. 1400–1600 | Common practice periods (mainly the era of the tonal system): Baroque: 1600–1750 | Classical: 1750–1820 | Romantic: c. 1800–1910 | 20th (modern) & 21st (contemporary) century`

Prof. Chae begins the historical survey by clarifying an essential vocabulary trap: in English, the word 'classical' is used in three completely different ways. First, 'classics' refers to any enduring masterpiece of high artistic value across generations (like Shakespeare in literature or Greek sculpture). Second, 'classical music' (broad sense) refers to the entire tradition of Western art music from the Middle Ages to the present day, as opposed to pop, rock, jazz, or folk music. Third, the 'Classical period' (narrow historical sense, 1750–1820) refers specifically to the 70-year era of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven.

Next, she maps out the seven major eras of Western music history and highlights a crucial umbrella term: the 'Common Practice Period' (c. 1600–1910), which encompasses the Baroque, Classical, and Romantic eras. Why are those three periods grouped together as the Common Practice Period? Because across those 300 years, Western composers all wrote using the Major/Minor tonal system (functional harmony based on major and minor scales), whereas Medieval and Renaissance music used church modes, and 20th-century modernists broke tonality into atonality.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **1. Classics vs. 2. Classical Music vs. 3. Classical Period**: (1) Classics = timeless masterpieces of any art; (2) Classical music = the entire Western art-music tradition; (3) The Classical Period = specifically 1750–1820 (Haydn, Mozart, Beethoven).
- **Pre-Tonal Eras**: Ancient Greece (antiquity) → Medieval (c. 1000–1400, though Gregorian chant begins c. 6th–10th C.) → Renaissance (c. 1400–1600).
- **Common Practice Period (1600–1910)**: The era governed by the Major/Minor tonal system: Baroque (1600–1750, ends with J. S. Bach's death in 1750) → Classical (1750–1820) → Romantic (c. 1800–1910).
- **Post-Tonal / Modern Era**: 20th (Modern) & 21st (Contemporary) century (c. 1890–present), when composers like Debussy, Stravinsky, and Schoenberg moved beyond traditional tonality.

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Know the exact boundary dates: Medieval (c. 1000–1400), Renaissance (c. 1400–1600), Baroque (1600–1750), Classical (1750–1820), Romantic (c. 1800–1910).
- Know that the 'Common Practice Period' means the era of the tonal system (Baroque + Classical + Romantic).

#### Slide 6 (p. 03) — Ancient Greek Music: Pythagoras's Hammers, Plato & Aristotle, and the 7 Greek Modes (Ancient Greece)
- **On the Slide**: `Ancient Greek music | Pythagoras (BC 580–500) | Plato, Aristotle: ideal, essential element of education, the good of humanity | Greek modes influence: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian | Blacksmith hammers story: weights 12, 9, 8, and 6 pounds -> 2:1 (octave), 12:9 = 4:3 (perfect fourth), 12:8 = 3:2 (perfect fifth), 9:8 (whole tone / whole step interval)`

Western music theory begins in Ancient Greece. Even though almost no notated Greek music survived (except fragments like the 1st-century Seikilos Epitaph), Greek philosophy and mathematics laid the foundation of Western music in three ways: (1) Acoustics & Tuning (Pythagoras), (2) Moral Philosophy of Music (Plato & Aristotle), and (3) The Modal Scale System (the 7 Greek Modes).

First, Prof. Chae explains the famous legend of Pythagoras (c. 580–500 BC) and the blacksmith's shop. Passing a forge, Pythagoras heard four hammers striking anvils. Some pairs rang out in pure harmony (consonance), while one pair clashed (dissonance). When he weighed the four hammers — A (12 lbs), B (9 lbs), C (8 lbs), and D (6 lbs) — he discovered that musical harmony is governed by exact mathematical integer ratios! Hammer A (12) and Hammer D (6) had a 2:1 ratio, producing the Octave (sounding like the same note at a higher pitch). Hammer A (12) and Hammer B (9) had a 12:9 = 4:3 ratio, producing the Perfect Fourth. Hammer A (12) and Hammer C (8) had a 12:8 = 3:2 ratio, producing the Perfect Fifth. However, Hammer B (9) and Hammer C (8) clashed in dissonance when struck together, and the ratio between them (9:8) defines the musical Whole Tone (whole step)! To illustrate pitch physics before this story, she also plays Henri Kling's 'The Elephant and the Fly' (piccolo = rapid vibration/high pitch vs. tuba/trombone = slow vibration/low pitch).

Second, Greek philosophers Plato and Aristotle taught the 'Doctrine of Ethos': because music directly shapes human character and the soul, music is an ideal, essential element of citizen education and must be used for the moral good of humanity.

Third, the Greeks organized melodies into 7 Modes (scales with different patterns of whole and half steps, which you can picture as starting a 7-note scale on each of the 7 white keys of a piano): Ionian (C), Dorian (D), Phrygian (E), Lydian (F), Mixolydian (G), Aeolian (A), and Locrian (B). In your lecture1.org notes, Prof. Chae specifically highlighted the Dorian Mode and played Simon & Garfunkel's 'Scarborough Fair' as a famous melody written in the Dorian mode!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Pythagoras (BC 580–500) & Hammer Ratios**: Hammers weighed 12, 9, 8, and 6 lbs. Ratios: 12:6 = 2:1 (Octave) | 12:9 = 4:3 (Perfect Fourth) | 12:8 = 3:2 (Perfect Fifth) | 9:8 (Whole Tone / Whole Step; dissonant when sounded together).
- **Plato & Aristotle (Philosophy of Music)**: Viewed music as an ideal, essential element of education that shapes human character and serves the moral good of humanity.
- **The 7 Greek Modes**: Ionian, Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian, Mixolydian, Aeolian, Locrian. Passed down into Medieval church modes; Ionian later became our Major scale and Aeolian became our natural Minor scale.
- **Dorian Mode Example (from lecture1.org)**: Traditional English ballad 'Scarborough Fair' (Simon & Garfunkel) is the classic example of the Dorian mode played in class.

#### Slide 7 (p. 04) — Medieval Sacred Music: Gregorian Chant & 'Dies Irae' (Medieval (c. 1000–1400))
- **On the Slide**: `1. Church music: Gregorian chants (6–10th century): origin of western music; Pope Gregory I; Latin; a cappella; male (monks); monophony; wo/notation, w/notation | Dies Irae: the day of wrath`

During the Middle Ages, the Roman Catholic Church was the supreme cultural and political authority in Europe, making sacred church music the documented origin of Western art music. Around the 6th century, Pope Gregory I (Pope Gregory the Great, r. 590–604) ordered the collection, codification, and standardization of sacred liturgical chants across the Catholic Church so that churches everywhere would sing the same unified repertoire — which is why they are called Gregorian Chants.

As you noted in lecture1.org, Prof. Chae emphasized six strict characteristics of Gregorian chant: (1) Sung in Latin (the official language of the Roman Catholic Church); (2) A cappella — literally meaning 'in the manner of the chapel', i.e., sung by voices alone because Pope Gregory I prohibited musical instruments in church worship as secular/pagan distractions; (3) Sung exclusively by males (monks and clergy) in monasteries and cathedrals (with the rare exception of convent nuns like Hildegard von Bingen); (4) Monophonic texture — a single, unified melodic line sung in unison with no harmony or chords; (5) Non-metric, free-flowing rhythm that follows the natural inflection of the Latin prayer text; and (6) Initially passed down orally without notation ('wo/notation'), which later forced monks to invent early musical notation ('w/notation') so chants wouldn't be forgotten.

The most famous Gregorian chant on your exam is 'Dies Irae' ('Day of Wrath', Listening Track #1), a 13th-century chant sequence from the Roman Catholic Requiem Mass (Mass for the Dead). Its ominous opening four-note descending motif became the universal musical symbol for death, doom, and judgment across cinema and classical music.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Gregorian Chant (Plainchant)**: The official sacred liturgical song of the Roman Catholic Church (6th–10th C.), regarded as the origin of Western art music.
- **Pope Gregory I**: Ordered the standardization and organization of church chants and forbade instruments in church worship.
- **A cappella**: Italian for 'in the style of the chapel' — vocal music performed without any instrumental accompaniment.
- **6 Core Traits of Gregorian Chant**: 1. Sacred Catholic prayer | 2. Latin text | 3. A cappella (no instruments) | 4. Sung by male monks | 5. Monophonic texture (one melody line) | 6. Evolved from oral tradition (without notation) to early written notation.
- **'Dies Irae' ('Day of Wrath', Track #1)**: Famous Gregorian chant from the Requiem Mass (Mass for the Dead) describing the Last Judgment. Quoted in Berlioz, Liszt, Verdi, and countless movie soundtracks.

#### Slide 8 (p. 05) — Medieval Secular Music: Troubadours, Trouvères, Meistersingers & Jongleurs (Medieval (c. 1000–1400))
- **On the Slide**: `2. Secular music | Troubadour: composer; performer; poet; Middle Ages (1100–1350); Trouvère, Meistersinger, Jongleur | * Italian music vs. French music`

Outside the walls of the monastery, a vibrant tradition of secular music (music NOT related to the Church) flourished in medieval castles, courts, and town squares between 1100 and 1350. Unlike Gregorian chant — which was sung in Latin by monks without instruments — medieval secular songs were sung in the local vernacular language (Old French, Occitan, German, Italian), accompanied by instruments (lute, harp, fiddle, flute, percussion), and had regular, dance-like rhythms.

Who created and performed this secular music? Noble poet-composer-performers! Depending on the region of Europe, they had different names that Prof. Chae expects you to recognize: Troubadours lived in Southern France (Provence); Trouvères lived in Northern France; and Minnesingers / Meistersingers lived in Germany. - **Meistersinger (Germany)**: German guild poet-singers and master musicians of the late Middle Ages and Renaissance.

#### Slide 9 (p. 06) — The Birth of Musical Notation: 9th-C. Neumes & Guido d'Arezzo (11th C.) (Medieval (c. 1000–1400))
- **On the Slide**: `Music (score) | Neuma (Neumes, 9th century): musical notation system * notes, musical notes | Guido d’Arezzo (11th century): use of solmization (ut-re-mi-fa-sol-la / hexachord system), standard music notation (using lines)`

How did Western music go from an oral tradition (memorized by ear) to written sheet music ('score')? As the repertory of Gregorian chants grew into the thousands, monks could no longer memorize every melody accurately. In the 9th century, scribes began writing small squiggly symbols called Neuma (Neumes) above the Latin words. However, early neumes only showed the general contour of the melody (whether the voice went up or down) — they could not show the exact pitch if you didn't already know the tune!

The decisive breakthrough came in the 11th century with an Italian Benedictine monk named Guido d'Arezzo (c. 991–1033), whom Prof. Chae (and your lecture1.org) calls the 'Father of Western Musical Notation'. Guido solved the pitch problem in two ways so singers could sight-read a piece they had never heard before: First, he arranged neumes on horizontal staff lines (a 4-line staff with colored lines for F and C, the direct ancestor of our modern 5-line staff) so vertical height showed exact pitch. Second, he invented Solmization ('ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la'), a 6-note Hexachord system taken from the first syllable of each line of the Latin Hymn to St. John the Baptist ('Ut queant laxis'). In the 17th century, 'Ut' was replaced by the more singable open syllable 'Do', and a 7th syllable 'Si/Ti' was added, creating our modern Do-Re-Mi-Fa-Sol-La-Ti scale! Also remember from lecture1.org: Western music divides the octave into 12 chromatic pitches (7 main natural notes + 5 half-step accidentals).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Neuma (Neumes, 9th Century)**: The earliest medieval musical notation symbols placed above chant lyrics to indicate melodic direction; evolved into square notes on a 4-line staff.
- **Guido d'Arezzo (11th Century)**: Italian monk revered as the 'Father of Western Musical Notation'. Invented staff-line pitch notation and the 6-note solmization system.
- **Solmization & Hexachord System**: Guido's 6-note sight-singing syllable system: ut–re–mi–fa–sol–la (derived from the Latin hymn 'Ut queant laxis'; 'ut' later became 'do').
- **12-Note Chromatic System (from lecture1.org)**: Western notation divides each octave into 12 equal semitones: 7 main natural notes (C D E F G A B, white keys) and 5 half-notes/accidentals (black keys).

#### Slide 10 (p. 07) — The Three Musical Textures: Monophony vs. Polyphony vs. Homophony (Medieval & Musical Fundamentals)
- **On the Slide**: `monophony vs. polyphony vs. homophony | organum`

Slide 10 teaches one of the most heavily tested concepts in the entire course: Musical Texture — how melodic and harmonic lines are woven together at any moment in a piece of music. Prof. Chae compares three textures using both visual line diagrams (shown in the slide image) and famous musical clips:

1. Monophony ('one sound'): A single unaccompanied melodic line with zero harmony or accompaniment. Even if 100 people sing that exact same melody together in unison, it is still monophonic! Examples: Gregorian chant (#1 'Dies Irae') and the opening vocal verse of Queen's 'We Will Rock You'.

2. Polyphony ('many sounds' / Counterpoint): Two or more independent melodies sounding at the same time, each with its own rhythm and contour, weaving around one another as equals. Examples: Medieval Organum, Renaissance motets (Josquin #2), and Baroque fugues and canons (Pachelbel's Canon, J. S. Bach).

3. Homophony ('same sound'): Either (a) one clear main melody supported by chordal accompaniment (like Chopin's Waltz or a Classical piano sonata with Alberti bass), or (b) multiple voices singing different pitches that harmonize together in the exact same rhythm (chordal homophony, like Martin Luther's church chorales or the 'Kingdom of this world' section in Handel's Hallelujah Chorus).

Finally, she plays the educational breakdown of Handel's 'Hallelujah' Chorus from Messiah because Handel brilliantly alternates between all three textures — Monophony ('For the Lord God omnipotent reigneth' in unison), Homophony (block chords on 'Hallelujah!'), and Polyphony ('And He shall reign for ever and ever' imitative fugal entries) — inside a single 4-minute piece!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Monophony (Monophonic)**: A single melodic line without accompaniment or harmony (e.g., Gregorian chant).
- **Polyphony (Polyphonic / Counterpoint)**: Two or more independent melodic lines played or sung simultaneously (e.g., Organum, Canon, Fugue, Josquin, Palestrina, J. S. Bach).
- **Homophony (Homophonic)**: One principal melody supported by harmonic chords, OR multiple voices moving in the exact same rhythm to form chords (e.g., Lutheran Chorale, Classical Sonata, Chopin Waltz).

#### Slide 11 (p. 08) — Organum: The Evolution of Early Church Polyphony (9th–14th C.) (Medieval (c. 1000–1400))
- **On the Slide**: `organum (polyphony) w/ 2 voices vs. Pérotin’s 4 voices (12th C.) vs. G. de Machaut’s 4 voices (14th C.)`

How did Western music transition from 1-voice monophonic Gregorian chant to multi-voice polyphony? That transition is called Organum (beginning in the 9th century). Monks began taking an existing Gregorian chant melody (held in long, slow notes in the bottom voice) and adding a second melodic line above it moving in parallel fourths or fifths ('2-voice organum').

By the 12th century, at the newly built Cathedral of Notre Dame in Paris (the Notre Dame School of Polyphony), the composer Pérotin (active c. 1200) revolutionized church music by expanding organum from 2 voices to 4 voices (' organum quadruplum', as in his 'Sederunt Principes'). Above a single ultra-slow chant note held like a drone at the bottom, three upper voices danced in lively rhythmic patterns.

Two centuries later, in the 14th century (the 'Ars Nova' or 'New Art' era), the French poet-composer Guillaume de Machaut (c. 1300–1377) composed the 'Messe de Nostre Dame' (Mass of Our Lady) — the first complete setting of the Catholic Mass Ordinary written for 4 independent polyphonic voices by a single named composer, paving the way directly for the Renaissance.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Organum (9th–13th C.)**: The earliest form of Western polyphonic church music, created by adding one or more contrapuntal voices above an existing Gregorian chant.
- **Pérotin (12th Century, Notre Dame School)**: Pioneered 4-voice organum (organum quadruplum) at Notre Dame Cathedral in Paris (e.g., 'Sederunt Principes').
- **Guillaume de Machaut (14th Century)**: Leading French Ars Nova composer; wrote the 4-voice 'Messe de Nostre Dame', the earliest complete polyphonic Mass by a single composer.

**Class Video Clips & Listening Links on This Slide:**

- [Class Video Clip] **Pérotin: 'Sederunt Principes' (4-Voice Organum, 12th C.)** (`https://youtu.be/EMyWnCf2Anc`) — Demonstrates 12th-century Notre Dame School 4-voice organum.
- [Class Video Clip] **Guillaume de Machaut: 'Messe de Nostre Dame' (14th C.)** (`https://youtu.be/1gEV42RKf6E`) — Demonstrates complex 14th-century 4-voice sacred polyphony.

#### Slide 12 (p. 09) — The Renaissance Period: Sacred vs. Secular Vocal Genres (Renaissance (c. 1400–1600))
- **On the Slide**: `Renaissance period (c. 1400–1600) | Italian & French church and secular music -> 15th century + British, Dutch music, other characteristics | Sacred & Secular: mass, motet / frottola, chanson, madrigal* | Organum: polyphony (9th century~); early polyphonic music`

The Renaissance ('Rebirth', c. 1400–1600) marked a cultural shift from strict medieval church scholasticism toward Humanism, the revival of Ancient Greek and Roman ideals, the invention of the Gutenberg printing press (which allowed sheet music to be printed and distributed across Europe), and a fuller, sweeter 4-part vocal polyphony where British and Franco-Flemish (Dutch/Belgian/Northern French) composers joined Italian and French traditions.

On Slide 12, Prof. Chae draws a sharp line between Renaissance Sacred vocal genres and Renaissance Secular vocal genres — a classic exam classification question! The two Sacred genres (sung in Latin for the church) are the Mass (a polyphonic setting of the 5 permanent prayers of the Catholic Mass: Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei) and the Motet (a sacred polyphonic choral work set to a Latin religious text other than the Mass Ordinary, such as Josquin's 'Ave Maria'). The three Secular genres (sung in the local vernacular language at court or home) are the Frottola (a light, rhythmic Italian court song that preceded the madrigal), the Chanson (French polyphonic secular song), and — most important of all, marked with an asterisk (*) on her slide — the Madrigal.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Renaissance Sacred Genres: Mass & Motet**: Both are sacred, a cappella, Latin polyphonic vocal works. Mass = sets the 5 liturgical prayers (Kyrie, Gloria, Credo, Sanctus, Agnus Dei); Motet = sets any other sacred Latin text (e.g., Josquin's 'Ave Maria').
- **Renaissance Secular Genres: Frottola, Chanson, Madrigal***: Secular courtly vocal works in the vernacular: Frottola (early Italian strophic song), Chanson (French secular polyphonic song), and Madrigal* (16th-C. Italian expressive polyphonic lyric song).
- **15th–16th C. Geographic Expansion**: Built upon earlier Italian & French music by integrating British ('contenance angloise' sweet 3rds and 6ths) and Franco-Flemish (Dutch) counterpoint.

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Be ready to sort Renaissance genres instantly: Sacred = Mass, Motet | Secular = Frottola, Chanson, Madrigal.

#### Slide 16 (p. 13) — The Baroque Era (1600–1750): Etymology, Doctrine of Affections & Figured Bass (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Barroco -> misshapen pearl | Baroque: Affection (vs. emotion -> Romantic era), straightforward; homophony; tonal system; ornamentations; Instrumental music; various secular music (+ sacred music); figured bass | Baroque period (1600–1750)`

Slide 16 introduces the Baroque Period (1600–1750) — starting around 1600 with the invention of Opera in Italy and ending in 1750 with the death of J. S. Bach. First, know the etymology: 'Baroque' comes from the Portuguese word 'barroco', a jewelers' term meaning an 'irregularly shaped or misshapen pearl'. Critics in the mid-18th century originally used the word as an insult, claiming that Baroque art, architecture (like Versailles or Bernini's sculptures shown on the slide), and music were overly ornate, theatrical, and exaggerated compared to Renaissance symmetry.

Next, Prof. Chae explains the 7 defining musical characteristics of the Baroque era on Slide 16:

1. The Doctrine of Affections ('Affection' vs. Romantic 'Emotion'): Why does she write 'Affection (vs. emotion -> Romantic era), straightforward'? Because in the 19th-century Romantic era, composers poured out their own turbulent, shifting personal diary emotions inside a single movement. By contrast, Baroque composers followed the philosophical 'Doctrine of Affections': they believed each musical movement should project ONE unified, objective, straightforward emotional state ('affection' — such as joy, grief, rage, or nobility) from beginning to end!

2. Shift to Homophony (in Early Baroque) → Culmination of Counterpoint (in Late Baroque): Around 1600, early Baroque pioneers rebelled against complex Renaissance polyphony in favor of clear homophony (one solo voice over chordal accompaniment, called Monody) so dramatic words could be understood, before late Baroque masters like Bach fused harmony and polyphony back together.

3. Establishment of the Major/Minor Tonal System (replacing the old church modes).

4. Lavish Ornamentation: Performers and composers decorated melodies with rapid trills, mordents, turns, and improvisatory flourishes (just like ornate Baroque gold architecture).

5. Rise of Independent Instrumental Music: For the first time in history, instrumental music (concertos, sonatas, suites) became equal in importance to vocal music.

6. Figured Bass (also called Basso Continuo or Thoroughbass): The unmistakable heartbeat of Baroque music! Look at the score diagram on Slide 16: the composer writes out the bass line (played by a low melody instrument like cello, bassoon, or viola da gamba) with small numbers ('figures') underneath the notes. A keyboard player (harpsichord or organ) or lute player reads those numbers and improvises the chords above the bass line in real time!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Barroco ('Misshapen Pearl')**: Portuguese origin of the word 'Baroque', referring to the ornate, dramatic, heavily decorated aesthetic of 1600–1750.
- **Doctrine of Affections (vs. Romantic Emotion)**: Baroque aesthetic principle that a single movement should express one unified, objective emotional state ('affection') straightforwardly throughout, rather than the personal emotional swings of the Romantic era.
- **Figured Bass (Basso Continuo)**: A continuous bass line with numbers written underneath that tell the harpsichord, organ, or lute player which chords to improvise.

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Every single item on Slide 16 is prime MCQ material: Barroco = misshapen pearl; Affection (Baroque) vs. Emotion (Romantic); Figured Bass = Baroque.

#### Slide 17 (p. 14) — Birth of Opera (c. 1600): Caccini, Monody & Evolution of Opera Plots (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Vocal music | ① Opera (Gesamtkunstwerk -> 19th C. Wagner): Caccini; Monody; Mythical contents -> Biblical contents -> Human-centered`

Why is the year 1600 chosen as the start of the Baroque era? Because around 1600 in Florence, Italy, a group of humanist scholars, poets, and musicians (the Florentine Camerata, including Giulio Caccini) invented Opera! Trying to recreate the emotional power of Ancient Greek tragedy, they realized that if five voices sing different melodies at the same time (Renaissance polyphony), nobody can understand the dramatic speech of a single character.

Their solution was Monody: a single expressive solo vocal melody accompanied by simple chords on a lute or harpsichord (basso continuo). Giulio Caccini published his landmark collection 'Le nuove musiche' ('The New Music', 1602), featuring the solo song 'Amarilli, mia bella' (Listening Track #4).

Prof. Chae also notes two big-picture facts on Slide 17: First, Opera combines music, drama, poetry, acting, costumes, and stage scenery — which 19th-century German composer Richard Wagner later termed 'Gesamtkunstwerk' ('Total Work of Art'). Second, notice the arrow showing how opera storylines evolved over time: early Baroque operas began with Mythical contents (Greek/Roman myths like Orpheus and Apollo) → expanded to Biblical & Historical contents → and by the Classical era (Mozart) shifted to Human-centered stories about real everyday people, servants, and aristocrats!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Monody & Giulio Caccini (1551–1618)**: Monody = solo vocal melody with figured-bass accompaniment designed to express poetic text clearly. Pioneered by Caccini in 'Amarilli, mia bella' (Track #4).
- **Gesamtkunstwerk ('Total Work of Art')**: Term coined in the 19th century by Richard Wagner describing opera as the synthesis of all arts (music, theatre, poetry, visual staging).
- **Evolution of Opera Subjects**: Mythical contents (Early Baroque, e.g., Orfeo) → Biblical/Historical contents → Human-centered contents (Classical era).

#### Slide 18 (p. 15) — Inside an Opera: Aria vs. Recitativo & Monteverdi's «L'Orfeo» (Deus ex machina) (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `* Aria vs. Recitativo (telling a story musically, speech, set-up) | * Monteverdi’s «Orfeo»: deus ex machina`

How is a Baroque or Classical opera structured from scene to scene? Composers alternate between two contrasting types of solo singing shown on Slide 18: Recitativo (Recitative) and Aria.

1. Recitativo ('speech-singing'): When characters need to argue, exchange dialogue, deliver news, or move the plot forward ('telling a story musically, speech, set-up'), they sing in Recitativo. The rhythm follows natural speech, pitches are repeated rapidly, and the accompaniment is minimal (often just dry chords on the harpsichord + cello).

2. Aria ('air / song'): Once the plot situation is set up by the recitativo, time freezes and the character steps forward to pour out their inner emotional reaction in an Aria — a fully melodic, structured song accompanied by the full orchestra, with soaring phrases and virtuosic vocal ornamentation.

Next, Prof. Chae highlights Claudio Monteverdi's «L'Orfeo» (1607), the first great masterpiece in opera history. Based on the Greek myth of Orpheus descending into Hades to rescue his bride Eurydice through the power of music, Monteverdi's opera ends with a 'deus ex machina' ('god from the machine'): in the original myth Orpheus is torn apart, but in Monteverdi's 1607 court opera, the god Apollo literally descends from the heavens on a mechanical cloud machine to rescue Orfeo and carry him to the stars for a happy ending!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Recitativo (Recitative)**: Speech-like singing with sparse accompaniment that advances the action/plot and sets up the dramatic situation ('telling a story musically').
- **Aria**: A self-contained, lyrical, emotional solo song with full orchestral accompaniment where a character reflects on their feelings.
- **Claudio Monteverdi's «L'Orfeo» (1607) & Deus ex machina**: First great operatic masterpiece; features a 'deus ex machina' ('god from the machine') ending where the god Apollo descends from heaven to resolve the plot.

**Class Video Clips & Listening Links on This Slide:**

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Never mix up Recitativo and Aria: Recitativo = speech-like dialogue that tells the story/plot; Aria = lyrical song expressing emotion.
- Connect Monteverdi's «Orfeo» with 'deus ex machina'.

#### Slide 19 (p. 16) — The Three Baroque Vocal Genres Compared: Opera vs. Oratorio vs. Cantata (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `② Oratorio (sacred) vs. opera — period instruments ('For unto us a child is born') | ③ Cantata (sacred & secular) vs. opera — 14’~ recitativo and aria (Cantata BWV 208 'Sheep may safely graze'), arran. by E. Petri`

Slide 19 completes the triad of Baroque vocal genres: ① Opera, ② Oratorio, and ③ Cantata. All three use the exact same musical building blocks — solo singers singing recitatives and arias, vocal ensembles/choruses, and orchestral accompaniment — so how do you tell them apart on the exam?

• Opera: Secular drama performed in a theater WITH acting, costumes, stage sets, and scenery.

• Oratorio: A large-scale, purely SACRED musical drama (usually based on a Biblical story, and in Handel's England sung in English) for soloists, choir, and orchestra, performed in a concert hall or church WITHOUT acting, costumes, or stage scenery! Why did Oratorio emerge? Initially in Catholic Rome during Lent (when theatrical operas were banned), and later popularized in Protestant England by Handel (whose 'Messiah' #8 'For Unto Us a Child is Born' is on this slide).

• Cantata: A shorter, multi-movement vocal work (consisting of recitatives, arias, and chorales/choruses with instrumental accompaniment) performed WITHOUT staging, written for EITHER sacred church services (like Bach's Lutheran church cantatas, e.g., BWV 147 'Jesu, Joy of Man's Desiring' #14) OR secular aristocratic celebrations (like Bach's secular 'Hunting Cantata' BWV 208, which contains the famous soprano aria 'Sheep May Safely Graze' #13 at 14:00, accompanied by two wooden Baroque flutes!).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Opera vs. Oratorio vs. Cantata**: Opera = staged theatrical drama (acting + costumes + scenery) | Oratorio = large-scale SACRED Biblical work with NO acting/costumes/scenery (e.g., Handel's Messiah) | Cantata = shorter unstaged multi-movement vocal work that can be EITHER Sacred OR Secular (e.g., Bach BWV 147 [sacred] & BWV 208 [secular]).
- **Handel's «Messiah» — 'For Unto Us a Child is Born' (Track #8)**: Sacred English Oratorio chorus; alternates sparkling polyphonic melismas ('born—') with grand homophonic chordal proclamations ('Wonderful! Counsellor!').
- **J. S. Bach's Cantata BWV 208 — 'Sheep May Safely Graze' (Track #13, 14:00~)**: From a SECULAR birthday cantata ('Hunting Cantata'); at 14:00 you hear a brief dramatic recitativo followed by the pastoral soprano aria accompanied by two wooden recorders/flutes.

#### Slide 20 (p. 17) — Baroque Instrumental Music ①: Concerto & Concerto Grosso (Tutti vs. Solo) (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Instrumental music | ① Concerto: compete (tutti vs. solo); contrast; concerto grosso | Brandenburg concerto no. 5 | Brandenburg no. 3 | vs. Rachmaninoff concerto (3’~), Tchaikovsky concerto (30’)`

Starting on Slide 20, Prof. Chae shifts from Baroque vocal genres to the four great Baroque instrumental genres: ① Concerto, ② Sonata, ③ Variations, and ④ Dance Suite. First up is the Concerto.

What does the word 'Concerto' mean? It carries the dual sense of 'working together' and 'competing / contending'. The fundamental principle of a concerto is dramatic CONTRAST and competition between two unequal forces: the Tutti (also called the Ripieno — the full orchestra playing together) and the Solo (the featured soloist or small solo group).

In the Baroque era, there were two types of concerto: (1) the Solo Concerto (one solo instrument, like a violin in Vivaldi's Four Seasons, competing against the orchestra), and (2) the Concerto Grosso ('big concerto'), which is uniquely Baroque! In a Concerto Grosso, instead of a single soloist, a small group of soloists (called the concertino) competes against the full orchestra (tutti). On Slide 20, Prof. Chae plays J. S. Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 in D Major, BWV 1050 (Listening Track #12) — a Concerto Grosso where a solo group of three instruments (Flute, Violin, and a dazzlingly virtuosic Harpsichord) dialogues with the string orchestra — alongside Brandenburg Concerto No. 3 in G Major. She then contrasts these intimate Baroque concerti grossi with two massive 19th-century Romantic solo piano concertos (Rachmaninoff's Piano Concerto No. 2 and Tchaikovsky's Piano Concerto No. 1) so you can hear how the small Baroque court ensemble later grew into a thundering 100-piece Romantic symphony orchestra.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Concerto (Core Principle)**: Competition and timbral/dynamic contrast between the Tutti (full orchestra) and the Solo.
- **Concerto Grosso (Baroque Specialty)**: A Baroque concerto where a small group of soloists (concertino) dialogues and competes with the full orchestra (tutti/ripieno).
- **Bach: Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, BWV 1050 (Track #12)**: Famous Baroque Concerto Grosso featuring a 3-instrument solo group (Flute + Violin + Harpsichord) and an unprecedented solo harpsichord cadenza.

#### Slide 21 (p. 18) — Baroque Instrumental Music ②–④: Sonata da chiesa vs. Sonata da camera, Variations & Dance Suite (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Instrumental music | ② Sonata (vs. cantata) | * sonata da chiesa -> solo sonata (classical era), Corelli’s trio sonata | * sonata da camera -> suite | ③ Variations | ④ Suite: Dance — Allemande, Sarabande, Gigue, Minuet`

On Slide 21, Prof. Chae explains the Baroque Sonata, Variations, and Dance Suite. First, why does she write 'Sonata (vs. cantata)'? Because from the Italian verbs 'suonare' (to sound/play on an instrument) and 'cantare' (to sing), a Cantata is a work to be SUNG, whereas a Sonata is a work to be PLAYED ON INSTRUMENTS!

In the Baroque era (perfected by Italian violinist-composer Arcangelo Corelli in his famous Trio Sonatas for 2 violins + basso continuo), there were two distinct types of Sonata that evolved in two different directions:

• Sonata da chiesa ('Church Sonata'): A serious, dignified multi-movement instrumental work (typically Slow–Fast–Slow–Fast, rich in counterpoint) suitable for church performance. As the arrow on Slide 21 shows, the Sonata da chiesa evolved directly into the Classical Solo Sonata!

• Sonata da camera ('Chamber Sonata'): A secular courtly work consisting of a sequence of stylized dance movements performed in aristocratic drawing rooms. As the arrow shows, the Sonata da camera evolved into (and merged with) the Baroque Dance Suite!

What is a Baroque Suite (④)? It is a multi-movement instrumental work made up of stylized international court dances, all in the same key, contrasting in tempo and meter. Even though nobody actually danced to a Bach cello suite or harpsichord suite in concert, each movement kept the rhythmic character of its national dance origin: (1) Allemande (German, moderate 4/4 time), (2) Courante (French, lively triple time), (3) Sarabande (Spanish via Mexico, slow and stately triple time with weight on the 2nd beat), (4) optional aristocratic dances like the Minuet (French, elegant 3/4 time), Gavotte, or Bourrée, and (5) Gigue (English/Irish 'jig', a fast, rollicking compound-meter 6/8 or 12/8 finale).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Sonata vs. Cantata**: Sonata (from 'suonare') = instrumental chamber work; Cantata (from 'cantare') = vocal work.
- **Sonata da chiesa ('Church Sonata')**: Dignified, abstract Baroque instrumental sonata (e.g., Corelli's Trio Sonatas) → evolved into the Classical Solo Sonata.
- **Sonata da camera ('Chamber Sonata')**: Secular courtly sonata built from stylized dance movements → evolved into the Baroque Dance Suite.
- **Baroque Dance Suite Movements**: Allemande (German, moderate 4/4) | Sarabande (Spanish, slow & solemn 3/4) | Minuet (French courtly 3/4 dance) | Gigue (British/Irish fast 6/8 jig finale).

#### Slide 22 (p. 19) — Baroque Period Instruments (Part 1): Viola da gamba, Baroque Violin Bow, Wooden Flute & Natural Trumpet (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Instruments: viola da gamba -> violin, violoncello (= cello), etc., lute, wind instruments (woodwind, brass), keyboard instruments (organ, harpsichord, clavichord, etc.) | ① Viola da gamba | ② Violin (bows) | ③ Flute (2:30~, 3:00~) | ④ Trumpet (4:00~, 6:00~)`

Why do Baroque recordings on 'period instruments' (historically informed performance) sound so different from a modern symphony orchestra? On Slides 22 and 23, Prof. Chae examines the six signature Baroque instruments and compares them directly against their modern descendants:

① Viola da gamba ('leg viol') → replaced by the Violoncello (Cello): The Viola da gamba was a bowed string instrument held upright between the knees/calves ('gamba' = leg in Italian). Unlike the cello, it had 6 or 7 gut strings, guitar-like frets on the fingerboard, a flat back, sloped shoulders, and was bowed with an underhand grip. Its tone was gentle, silvery, and intimate — ideal for small Renaissance and Baroque chambers, but too quiet for large concert halls, which is why the louder, fretless 4-string Violoncello (Cello) eventually replaced it.

② Baroque Violin & Convex Baroque Bow: A Baroque violin used sheep-gut strings (instead of modern steel-core strings), had no chinrest or shoulder rest, and was played with a shorter, outward-curving (convex) Baroque bow. Because of that bow shape, notes naturally swelled in the middle and tapered off gently at the tip, creating an articulate, 'speaking' phrasing — which Prof. Chae demonstrates by comparing Shunsuke Sato (playing Bach's Chaconne with a historical Baroque bow) against Korean virtuoso Kyung-Wha Chung (playing with a modern concave Tourte bow).

③ Baroque Flute (Traverso): Made of wood (boxwood or ebony) with simple finger holes and only one metal key, producing a warm, breathy, pastoral sound compared to the loud, brilliant modern silver Boehm flute.

④ Baroque Natural Trumpet: A long coiled brass tube with NO valves or pistons! Because there were no valves, the trumpeter could only play the natural harmonic overtone series by changing lip tension (embouchure) and air speed — forcing melodies into the stratospheric high 'clarino' register where notes of the harmonic series lie close together.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **① Viola da gamba vs. Violoncello (Cello)**: Viola da gamba = 6/7-string fretted bowed viol held between the legs with an underhand bow grip; later replaced by the louder, fretless 4-string cello.
- **② Baroque Violin & Bow**: Gut strings, no chinrest, and a lighter outward-curved (convex) bow that produces natural articulation and decay.
- **③ Baroque Flute**: Made of wood (not metal/silver) with a soft, woody, breathy tone.
- **④ Baroque Natural Trumpet**: Valveless brass tube; players produced pitches purely through lip pressure in the high harmonic ('clarino') register.

#### Slide 23 (p. 20) — Baroque Period Instruments (Part 2): Pipe Organ, Harpsichord (Clavecin / Cembalo / Virginal) & Clavichord (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `⑤ Pipe organ | ⑥ Keyboard: harpsichord (= clavecin, cembalo, virginal), clavichord`

Slide 23 covers the three Baroque keyboard instruments — a favorite exam topic because the mechanical difference between them explains why the invention of the Piano (Fortepiano) in the Classical period was such a revolution!

⑤ Pipe Organ ('King of Instruments'): Wind blown through hundreds or thousands of metal and wooden pipes controlled by multiple hand keyboards (manuals), stops (which change timbre), and a full foot pedalboard. In J. S. Bach's hands (#20 Toccata and Fugue in D Minor), the pipe organ could fill a massive cathedral with sustained polyphony.

⑥ Harpsichord (= called 'Clavecin' in French, 'Cembalo' in Italian/German, and 'Virginal' for the smaller rectangular tabletop version): How does a harpsichord produce sound? When you press a key, a wooden jack rises and a small plectrum (quill or leather pick) PLUCKS the metal string! Because the string is plucked mechanically, the harpsichord has a brilliant, metallic, snapping sound — BUT it CANNOT play louder or softer depending on how hard or softly your finger presses the key! (This inability to make gradual crescendos by finger touch is the #1 exam fact about the harpsichord, and why you must memorize its three synonyms: Harpsichord = Clavecin = Cembalo, plus Virginal.)

⑥ Clavichord: A small, rectangular domestic keyboard instrument where pressing a key causes a small brass blade (tangent) to STRIKE and stay pressed against the string. Unlike the harpsichord, the clavichord CAN vary volume slightly and even produce vibrato ('Bebung'), and J. S. Bach & C. P. E. Bach loved practicing on it at home — but its sound is so whisper-quiet that it cannot be heard in a concert hall.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **⑤ Pipe Organ**: Wind-powered cathedral keyboard instrument with multiple hand manuals, timbre stops, and a foot pedalboard.
- **⑥ Harpsichord (= Clavecin = Cembalo = Virginal)**: Strings are PLUCKED by quills when keys are pressed. Bright metallic tone; CANNOT vary volume (dynamics) by finger touch! Memorize its alternate names: Clavecin (French), Cembalo (Italian/German), Virginal (small rectangular model).
- **⑥ Clavichord**: Strings are STRUCK by small metal tangents. Capable of subtle dynamic nuance, but extremely quiet (for solo home practice only).

#### Slide 24 (p. 21) — The Tonal System (J. P. Rameau) & Composer Catalogue Systems (Op., BWV, K., Hob., D.) (Baroque & Music Theory)
- **On the Slide**: `Tonal system: scales and chords / Major and Minor | * J. P. Rameau «Treatise on Harmony» | Opus (Op.): 'work,' specifying order of (usually) publication | * BWV (Bach Werke Verzeichnis), K. (Köchel), Hob. (Hoboken), D. (Deutsch), etc.`

Slide 24 contains the exact material from Prof. Chae's Slide 3 sample midterm question! It covers two foundational pillars:

1. The Major/Minor Tonal System & Jean-Philippe Rameau: During the Baroque era, the old medieval church modes were consolidated into the Tonal System — music organized around a central home pitch ('tonic') using Major scales/chords (bright, stable) and Minor scales/chords (dark, tense, melancholic). In 1722, the French Baroque composer and theorist Jean-Philippe Rameau published his landmark book «Treatise on Harmony» ('Traité de l'harmonie'), which mathematically and theoretically codified chord progressions (tonic, dominant, subdominant) and chord inversions — laying the foundation of modern harmony! To show what 'tonal' vs. 'atonal' sounds like, Prof. Chae embeds Mozart's purely tonal C-major Sonata K. 545 next to Arnold Schoenberg's 20th-century atonal Suite Op. 25.

2. Musical Catalogue Systems: Why do some pieces have 'Op.' numbers while others have letters like 'BWV', 'K.', 'Hob.', or 'D.'? 'Opus' (Latin for 'work', abbreviated Op.) is assigned by a composer or publisher in chronological order of publication (for example, Beethoven's Symphony No. 5 is Op. 67). However, Baroque and early Classical composers like Bach, Mozart, Haydn, and Schubert did not systematically assign Opus numbers to all their manuscripts. Centuries later, musicologists created comprehensive thematic catalogues of their complete works, and each catalogue is abbreviated after the scholar or title:

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **J. P. Rameau & «Treatise on Harmony» (1722)**: French Baroque composer/theorist who codified the rules of Major/Minor functional harmony and chords in his 'Treatise on Harmony'.
- **Opus (Op.)**: Latin for 'work'; indicates the chronological order of publication (used systematically by Clementi, Kuhlau, and Beethoven).
- **BWV (Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis)**: Thematic catalogue of Johann Sebastian Bach's works ('Catalogue of Bach's Works', compiled by Wolfgang Schmieder).
- **K. or KV (Köchel Catalogue)**: Chronological catalogue of Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart's works (compiled by Ludwig von Köchel). *(Note: 'K.' on Domenico Scarlatti's sonatas stands for Ralph Kirkpatrick!)*
- **Hob. (Hoboken Catalogue)**: Thematic catalogue of Franz Joseph Haydn's works (compiled by Anthony van Hoboken).
- **D. (Deutsch Catalogue)**: Chronological catalogue of Romantic composer Franz Schubert's works (compiled by Otto Erich Deutsch).

#### Slide 25 (p. 22) — How to Read a Musical Score: Clef, Time Signature, Staff, Key Signature, Measure & Movements (Musical Score Fundamentals)
- **On the Slide**: `Clef: treble, bass, alto, etc. | Time Signature: 4/4 (C), 3/4, 2/4, 2/2, 6/8, 9/8, etc. | Staff, Grand staff | Key Signature: tonal music | Measure (bar) | Movements`

On Slide 25, Prof. Chae walks through the anatomy of a musical score (shown in the annotated diagram on the slide) so every student knows what each symbol at the start of a piece means:

• Staff & Grand Staff: The 5 horizontal lines and 4 spaces on which notes are written. Piano/keyboard music uses a Grand Staff — a top staff (Treble clef, usually played by the right hand) joined by a brace to a bottom staff (Bass clef, usually played by the left hand).

• Clef: The symbol at the far left of a staff that fixes the pitch of one specific line: Treble Clef (G clef, curls around the G line for higher pitches like violin, flute, right-hand piano), Bass Clef (F clef, two dots surround the F line for low pitches like cello, bassoon, left-hand piano), and Alto/Tenor Clef (C clef, used by the viola).

• Key Signature: The group of sharps (#) or flats (b) placed immediately after the clef. It tells the performer which Major or Minor key the tonal piece is written in (for example, 0 sharps/flats = C Major or A Minor; 1 flat = F Major or D Minor).

• Time Signature (Meter): The fraction placed after the key signature (such as 4/4 — also written as 'C' for Common Time — 3/4 waltz/minuet time, 2/4 march time, or 6/8 compound jig/pastoral time). The top number tells how many beats are in each measure; the bottom number tells which note value gets one beat.

• Measure (Bar): A single segment of time marked off by vertical barlines containing the number of beats specified by the time signature.

• Movements: Self-contained sections of a larger work (like a Symphony, Concerto, or Sonata), each having its own tempo, key, and form, separated by a brief pause in performance.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Clef (Treble, Bass, Alto)**: Symbol at the start of the staff that assigns specific pitches to the lines (Treble = G clef, Bass = F clef, Alto = C clef).
- **Key Signature**: Sharps (#) or flats (b) at the beginning of the staff indicating the Major or Minor key of tonal music.
- **Time Signature**: Numbers (e.g., 4/4 = C, 3/4, 6/8) indicating the meter (beats per measure and note value of each beat).
- **Staff / Grand Staff / Measure (Bar) / Movements**: Staff = 5 lines; Grand Staff = joined treble + bass staves for keyboard; Measure = unit between vertical barlines; Movements = independent chapters/sections of a multi-movement work.

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Do not confuse Key Signature (sharps/flats showing the tonal Major/Minor key) with Time Signature (4/4, 3/4, 6/8 showing the beat/meter).

#### Slide 26 (p. 23) — Italian Tempo & Dynamic Markings + Baroque 'Terraced Dynamics' (Baroque & Musical Terms)
- **On the Slide**: `Terms (Italian) | Tempo (character): Lento, Largo / Adagio / Andante / Moderato / Allegro / Vivace / Presto | Dynamics: f (forte) <-> p (piano), pp, ppp, mp, mf, ff, fff, sfz | crescendo, decrescendo = diminuendo | Baroque dynamics: terraced dynamics (abrupt changes in volume by introducing more instruments) — Brandenburg No. 5, 3rd mvt.`

Why are almost all tempo and dynamic markings in Western sheet music written in Italian? Because music printing, opera, and instrumental genres (sonata, concerto) were pioneered in 17th-century Italy! On Slide 26, Prof. Chae requires you to know the hierarchy of Italian Tempo markings (speed + character) from slowest to fastest, the Dynamic markings (volume), and the special Baroque principle of Terraced Dynamics:

1. Tempo Markings (Slowest → Fastest): Lento & Largo (very slow, broad, solemn) → Adagio (slow, leisurely, expressive — 'at ease') → Andante (moderately slow, 'walking pace') → Moderato (moderate speed) → Allegro (fast, bright, cheerful) → Vivace (lively,vivacious) → Presto (very fast, rapid).

2. Dynamic Markings: 'piano' (p) = soft; 'pianissimo' (pp) = very soft; 'mezzo-piano' (mp) = moderately soft; 'mezzo-forte' (mf) = moderately loud; 'forte' (f) = loud; 'fortissimo' (ff) = very loud; 'sforzando' (sfz) = a sudden, forceful accent on a single note or chord (like in Haydn's Surprise Symphony!). 'Crescendo' (<) = gradually getting louder; 'Decrescendo' or 'Diminuendo' (>) = gradually getting softer.

3. Baroque Terraced Dynamics: How did Baroque composers handle volume changes before the piano was invented? Because the harpsichord and organ could not make gradual finger-pressure crescendos, and because Baroque aesthetics favored clear contrast between solo and tutti, Baroque music uses Terraced Dynamics — abrupt, step-like shifts from loud (forte) to soft (piano) and back again, created by suddenly adding or subtracting instruments (as shown in the graphical score of Bach's Brandenburg Concerto No. 5, 3rd movement)!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Italian Tempo Order (Slow → Fast)**: Lento / Largo (very slow) → Adagio (slow) → Andante (walking pace) → Moderato (moderate) → Allegro (fast) → Vivace (lively) → Presto (very fast).
- **Italian Dynamic Terms**: p (piano = soft), f (forte = loud), mp/mf (moderately soft/loud), pp/ff (very soft/loud), sfz (sforzando = sudden strong accent), crescendo (gradually louder), decrescendo / diminuendo (gradually softer).
- **Terraced Dynamics (Baroque Hallmark)**: Abrupt, stair-step changes between loud (f) and soft (p) levels achieved by adding or removing instruments (tutti vs. solo), rather than gradual crescendos.

#### Slide 28 (p. 25) — The Baroque Superstar Voice: Castrato (Farinelli, Moreschi) vs. Modern Countertenor (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `* Castrato (Farinelli, Moreschi) vs. countertenor`

Why are the heroic male roles in Baroque Italian operas (such as Handel's 'Rinaldo' #9 and 'Xerxes' #10) written in the high soprano or alto range? On Slide 28, Prof. Chae explains the phenomenon of the Castrato and how those roles are performed today by Countertenors:

1. The Castrato (17th–18th C.): In Baroque Italy, the Catholic Church forbade women from singing in church choirs (and in the Papal States, even on the opera stage), yet audiences craved dazzling high voices. Boys with promising voices were castrated before puberty so their larynx (voice box) never dropped into a low adult male register. As they grew into adulthood, a castrato combined the high, pure vocal cords of a boy soprano/alto with the massive chest cavity, lung capacity, and physical stamina of a grown man! Castrati could hold a single breath for over a minute and execute superhuman coloratura runs, making them the rockstars and highest-paid celebrities of 18th-century Baroque 'opera seria'.

Prof. Chae highlights two historical castrati on Slide 28: (a) Farinelli (Carlo Broschi, 1705–1782), the most famous castrato superstar of the 18th century (depicted in the 1994 movie 'Farinelli', where engineers digitally blended a male countertenor and a female soprano to recreate his voice); and (b) Alessandro Moreschi (1858–1922), the 'Angel of Rome' in the Sistine Chapel Choir — the LAST castrato in history and the only one ever captured on audio recording (in 1902–1904)!

2. The Modern Countertenor: Because castration was outlawed in the late 19th century, who sings Baroque castrato roles today (besides female mezzo-sopranos)? A Countertenor! A countertenor is a completely natural adult male singer who trains his falsetto / head-voice register to sing in the alto or soprano range without any surgery. On your official exam listening list, both Handel opera arias — #9 'Lascia ch'io pianga' (sung by French countertenor Philippe Jaroussky) and #10 'Ombra mai fu' (sung by German countertenor Andreas Scholl) — are performed by world-famous modern countertenors! Prof. Chae also played Korean countertenor Lee Dong-kyu on Phantom Singer 4.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Castrato**: Male singer castrated before puberty to preserve a soprano/alto voice with adult male lung capacity. Superstar heroes of Baroque opera.
- **Farinelli (1705–1782) & Alessandro Moreschi (1858–1922)**: Farinelli = legendary 18th-C. castrato virtuoso; Moreschi = the last castrato in history (recorded in 1902 at the Vatican).
- **Countertenor (vs. Castrato)**: A modern male singer who sings in the high alto/soprano range naturally using falsetto/head-voice technique (e.g., Philippe Jaroussky on Track #9, Andreas Scholl on Track #10, Lee Dong-kyu).

#### Slide 29 (p. 26) — Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741, Italy): 'The Four Seasons' & Violin Concertos (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741, Italy) | violin concertos | Gloria | Winter 1st, 2nd mvt. (Clara-Jumi Kang, Portrait of a Lady on Fire, Yanni, John Wick) | Autumn 3rd mvt. | Spring, La Primavera 1st mvt.`

Slide 29 begins the deep dive into the four major Baroque composers (Vivaldi, Telemann/Scarlatti, J. S. Bach, and Handel). First is Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741), born in Venice, Italy. Ordained as a Catholic priest and blessed with shockingly bright red hair, Vivaldi was universally nicknamed 'Il Prete Rosso' ('The Red Priest'). For most of his career, he worked as violin master and composer in residence at the Ospedale della Pietà — a Venetian convent, orphanage, and music conservatory for girls whose all-female orchestra became legendary across Europe.

Vivaldi composed over 500 concertos (more than 230 for solo violin!), sacred choral works like his radiant 'Gloria', and operas. He standardized the 3-movement Venetian Concerto structure (Fast – Slow – Fast) and perfected Ritornello Form in the fast movements: the full orchestra (Tutti) opens with a recurring main theme ('Ritornello'), which alternates with flashy, virtuosic episodes played by the solo violin.

His supreme masterpiece is «The Four Seasons» ('Le quattro stagioni', Op. 8 Nos. 1–4, 1725) — four solo violin concertos depicting Spring, Summer, Autumn, and Winter, each accompanied by an Italian descriptive sonnet (making it an early milestone of Baroque Program Music, even though the term 'program music' was coined in the 19th century). THREE movements from The Four Seasons are on your 49-track listening list, and you MUST not mix them up:

• Track #5 — 'Winter' (L'Inverno), 1st Mvt (Allegro non molto, F minor): Opens with icy, shivering tremolos stacked note-by-note in the strings before the solo violin erupts in fierce, biting gusts of freezing wind (featured in 'Portrait of a Lady on Fire' and 'John Wick: Chapter 3').

• Track #7 — 'Spring' (La Primavera), 1st Mvt (Allegro, E major): The most famous melody in Baroque music — bright, joyous repeated-note spring fanfare answered by high violin trills imitating singing birds.

#### Slide 31 (p. 28) — Georg Philipp Telemann (Tafelmusik) & Domenico Scarlatti (555 Harpsichord Sonatas) (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `G. P. Telemann (1681–1767, Germany): Tafelmusik; public concert | D. Scarlatti (1685–1757, Italy): harpsichord sonatas (K. 141, K. 159, K. 1 – 2:25~)`

Slide 31 introduces two major Baroque masters whose careers contrasted sharply with J. S. Bach's:

1. Georg Philipp Telemann (1681–1767, Germany): During their lifetimes, Telemann was actually far more famous in Germany than his friend J. S. Bach (in fact, Leipzig only hired Bach after Telemann turned the job down!). Telemann is remembered on the exam for two key contributions: First, he composed «Tafelmusik» ('Table Music' / 'Musique de table', 1733), a lavish collection of orchestral suites, concertos, and chamber music designed to accompany grand aristocratic banquets and feasts. Second, Telemann pioneered early PUBLIC CONCERTS in Frankfurt and Hamburg — charging admission so the rising urban middle class (bourgeoisie) could hear concert music that had previously been locked inside royal palaces!

2. Domenico Scarlatti (1685–1757, Italy → Portugal/Spain): Born in Naples in 1685 — the EXACT same birth year as J. S. Bach and G. F. Handel! Scarlatti left Italy to serve Princess Maria Barbara of Portugal (later Queen of Spain) in Madrid, where he composed 555 single-movement Harpsichord Sonatas (catalogued with 'K.' numbers by Ralph Kirkpatrick — do NOT confuse Scarlatti's Kirkpatrick 'K.' numbers with Mozart's Köchel 'K.' numbers!). Influenced by Spanish folk music and flamenco guitars, Scarlatti's Harpsichord Sonata in D Minor, K. 141 (Listening Track #11) features explosive machine-gun repeated notes (imitating guitar strumming), wide leaps, and daring hand-crossing.

#### Slide 32 (p. 29) — Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750, Germany): Six Core Exam Keywords (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750, Germany) | counterpoint | conservative | BWV | German + Italian Baroque music | church musician | Mendelssohn`

Slides 32 through 37 (6 full slides!) are devoted to Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750), the towering culmination of the Baroque era (in fact, historians mark the end of the Baroque period with the year of Bach's death, 1750). On Slide 32, Prof. Chae lists six essential keywords that summarize Bach's life and historical legacy — and every single one of these six bullets is a potential multiple-choice question:

1. Counterpoint (Polyphony): Bach was the supreme master of polyphonic counterpoint — fugues, canons, and chorale preludes — weaving multiple independent voices together with mathematical perfection and deep spiritual expression.

2. 'Conservative': Why does Prof. Chae write 'conservative' on Slide 32? Because by the 1730s and 1740s, younger composers (including Bach's own sons, C. P. E. Bach and J. C. Bach!) were turning toward the light, simple, homophonic Pre-Classical 'Galant' style. They viewed J. S. Bach's dense, complex fugal counterpoint as old-fashioned, heavy, and 'conservative'!

3. BWV (Bach-Werke-Verzeichnis): His works are catalogued by BWV numbers (compiled by Wolfgang Schmieder in 1950), organized by genre rather than date.

4. Fusion of German + Italian (+ French) Baroque Music: Even though Bach never left Germany, he synthesized German Lutheran organ/chorale polyphony with the rhythmic drive and ritornello forms of Italian concertos (Vivaldi, Corelli, Marcello) and the elegance of French dance suites.

5. Devout Lutheran Church Musician: Unlike Handel (who was a cosmopolitan impresario writing Italian operas for London royalty), Bach spent his career as a provincial German court kapellmeister (Weimar, Köthen) and spent his final 27 years (1723–1750) as Cantor of St. Thomas Lutheran Church (Thomaskirche) in Leipzig, signing his scores 'S.D.G.' ('Soli Deo Gloria' — To God Alone the Glory).

6. Felix Mendelssohn's 1829 Revival: Because Bach was viewed as an old-fashioned church organist and contrapuntist when he died in 1750, most of his music went unpublished and was largely forgotten by the general public for nearly 80 years — until 1829, when the 20-year-old Romantic composer Felix Mendelssohn conducted a historic revival performance of Bach's 'St. Matthew Passion' in Berlin, sparking the worldwide Bach revival!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **1. Counterpoint & 2. Conservative**: Bach brought Baroque polyphony (fugue/canon) to its peak at a time when younger contemporaries already considered dense counterpoint 'conservative' and outdated.
- **3. BWV & 4. German + Italian Synthesis**: Catalogued as BWV; fused German Lutheran counterpoint with Italian concerto form (Vivaldi/Marcello) and French dance suites.
- **5. Lutheran Church Musician (vs. Handel)**: Worked in Germany (Weimar, Köthen, and St. Thomas Church in Leipzig) writing music for the Lutheran church and local courts.
- **6. Felix Mendelssohn (1829 Revival)**: 19th-century Romantic composer who rediscovered and conducted Bach's 'St. Matthew Passion' in 1829, rescuing Bach from obscurity.

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Memorize all 6 keywords on Slide 32 for J. S. Bach: Counterpoint | Conservative | BWV | German + Italian Baroque music | Lutheran Church Musician | Revived by Felix Mendelssohn (1829).

#### Slide 36 (p. 33) — J. S. Bach's Solo Masterworks: Goldberg Variations, Cello Suite No. 1 & Toccata and Fugue in D Minor (Baroque (1600–1750))

Slide 36 presents three of J. S. Bach's most iconic solo instrumental works — each written for a different solo instrument (Harpsichord, Solo Cello, and Pipe Organ) and all three on your 49-track listening list:

1. Goldberg Variations, BWV 988 — 'Aria' (Listening Track #18): Composed in 1741 for a two-manual harpsichord (and made famous on modern piano by Canadian pianist Glenn Gould in his legendary 1955 and 1981 recordings, as well as in the film 'The Silence of the Lambs' / 'Hannibal'). According to Bach's early biographer Forkel, the work was commissioned by Russian ambassador Count Kaiserling, who suffered from severe insomnia and asked his young resident harpsichordist Johann Gottlieb Goldberg to play Bach's soothing variations in the next room during his sleepless nights. 2. Suite for Solo Cello No. 1 in G Major, BWV 1007 — 'Prelude' (Listening Track #19): Before Bach, the cello was treated merely as a bass-line accompaniment instrument (basso continuo). In his 6 Cello Suites (composed in Köthen c. 1720 and rediscovered in a Barcelona thrift shop by cellist Pablo Casals in 1889), Bach proved that a single unaccompanied cello could imply full 4-part harmony through continuous rolling arpeggios (G–D–B–A–B–D–B–D).

3. Toccata and Fugue in D Minor, BWV 565 (Listening Track #20): The most famous Pipe Organ work in history! **Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Goldberg Variations, BWV 988 — 'Aria' (Track #18)**: A slow, heavily ornamented G-major Sarabande theme followed by 30 variations over a 32-note ground bass (linked with Count Kaiserling's insomniac harpsichordist Goldberg, Glenn Gould, and Hannibal Lecter).
- **Suite for Solo Cello No. 1 in G Major, BWV 1007 — Prelude (Track #19)**: Unaccompanied solo cello playing continuous, warm, wave-like 16th-note arpeggios.
- **Toccata and Fugue in D Minor, BWV 565 (Track #20)**: Monumental Pipe Organ work combining an improvisatory, dramatic Toccata ('to touch') with a strict contrapuntal 4-voice Fugue.

#### Slide 37 (p. 34) — J. S. Bach's «The Well-Tempered Clavier»: 'The Old Testament' of Keyboard Music (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `48 Well-Tempered Clavier (2 books) | 12 major keys & 12 minor keys; 'the old testament' | Prelude No. 1 in C Major, BWV 846 | Bobby McFerrin | Ave Maria (Gounod)`

Slide 37 is one of the most frequently tested slides on the entire midterm! What is J. S. Bach's «The Well-Tempered Clavier» ('Das Wohltemperierte Klavier', BWV 846–893)?

Before Bach's time, keyboard instruments were tuned in 'meantone temperament', which made simple keys (like C major or G major) sound pure, but made keys with many sharps or flats (like F# major or C# minor) sound painfully out of tune ('wolf intervals'). With the introduction of 'well temperament' (which slightly adjusted the tuning across the 12 semitones of the octave), a single keyboard instrument ('Clavier' = generic German term for any keyboard: harpsichord, clavichord, or organ) could play in ALL 24 keys!

To prove this and provide a complete pedagogical encyclopedia for his students, Bach composed TWO Books (Book I in 1722, Book II in 1742). Each book contains 24 pairs of Preludes and Fugues moving chromatically up through all 12 Major keys and all 12 Minor keys (C major, C minor, C# major, C# minor... up to B minor). Because 24 × 2 books = 48 Preludes and Fugues in total, it is often called 'The 48'!

Crucial Exam Nickname (coined by 19th-century conductor Hans von Bülow): Bach's 48 Preludes & Fugues («The Well-Tempered Clavier») are called **'The Old Testament'** of piano/keyboard music, whereas Beethoven's 32 Piano Sonatas (PPT 2 Slide 25) are called **'The New Testament'**! Finally, note the famous connection on Slide 37: over a century after Bach wrote Prelude No. 1 in C Major (Listening Track #16), 19th-century French composer Charles Gounod wrote a soaring soprano melody ('Ave Maria') Directly on top of Bach's unaltered C-major arpeggios — which jazz vocalist Bobby McFerrin famously got an entire concert hall audience to sing from memory while he sang Bach's arpeggios!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **«The Well-Tempered Clavier» (BWV 846–893)**: 2 Books × 24 pairs (12 Major keys + 12 Minor keys per book) = 48 Preludes and Fugues in total.
- **'The Old Testament' vs. 'The New Testament'**: Old Testament of keyboard music = J. S. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier (48 Preludes & Fugues) | New Testament of keyboard music = Beethoven's 32 Piano Sonatas.
- **Prelude No. 1 in C Major, BWV 846 (Track #16) & Gounod's 'Ave Maria'**: Opens Book 1 with serene, unbroken rising 16th-note arpeggios; later used by Charles Gounod as the exact accompaniment for the Bach/Gounod 'Ave Maria'.

#### Slide 38 (p. 35) — George Frideric Handel (1685–1759, Germany → England): Cosmopolitan Master of Opera & Oratorio (Baroque (1600–1750))
- **On the Slide**: `George Frideric Handel (1685–1759, Germany -> England) | popular; royal musician; cosmopolitan; homophony | Oratorio (Messiah) and Opera (Xerxes — 'Largo' Ombra mai fu)`

Slides 38 and 39 conclude PPT 1 with George Frideric Handel (1685–1759) — born in Halle, Germany in the exact same year (1685) and just a few miles away from J. S. Bach, yet the two men never met and lived completely opposite lives! Look at the four profile keywords Prof. Chae puts on Slide 38 to contrast Handel directly against Bach (Slide 32):

1. Cosmopolitan (Germany → Italy → England): While Bach never left provincial Germany, Handel trained in Hamburg, traveled across Italy soaking up brilliant Italian opera and melody, and in 1712 settled permanently in London, England (becoming a naturalized British citizen).

2. Royal Musician: While Bach worked for Lutheran town councils and churches, Handel served the British Royal Family (King George I and King George II) in London.

3. Popular (Entrepreneur & Public Appeal): Handel ran commercial opera companies in London and wrote theatrical music designed to thrill the public.

4. Homophony (Grand Dramatic Clarity): While Bach was the conservative master of dense contrapuntal polyphony, Handel favored direct, theatrical, emotionally immediate Homophony (though in his oratorio choruses like 'For Unto Us a Child is Born' #8 and 'Hallelujah', he alternated rousing fugal polyphony with massive homophonic block chords for maximum dramatic punch!).

Why did Handel switch from Italian Opera to English Oratorio? In the 1710s–1720s, Handel conquered London with Italian Operas ('opera seria') starring superstar castrati, such as «Rinaldo» (1711, containing Listening Track #9 'Lascia ch'io pianga' — 'Let me weep over my cruel fate', a heartbreakingly slow Sarabande aria) and «Xerxes» ('Serse', 1738, containing Listening Track #10 'Ombra mai fu', famously known as Handel's 'Largo' — a tender aria where Persian King Xerxes sings his love to the shade of a plane tree!). However, by the 1730s, English middle-class audiences grew tired of expensive Italian operas they couldn't understand. Facing bankruptcy, Handel reinvented the English Oratorio — unstaged Biblical dramas sung in English (requiring no costly Italian castrati or stage sets!), culminating in «Messiah» (1741, Track #8 'For Unto Us a Child is Born', plus 'Hallelujah' and 'Worthy is the Lamb').

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Handel vs. Bach (Exam Comparison)**: Bach = stayed in Germany, Lutheran church musician, conservative counterpoint/polyphony, BWV | Handel = Germany → Italy → England, cosmopolitan royal musician, popular dramatic homophony, Italian Operas + English Oratorios (HWV).
- **Handel's Oratorio: «Messiah» (Track #8)**: Sacred English oratorio (1741) performed without staging; includes 'For Unto Us a Child is Born' (#8), 'Hallelujah', and 'Worthy is the Lamb'.
- **Handel's Operas: «Rinaldo» (Track #9) & «Xerxes» (Track #10)**: Italian Baroque operas: #9 'Lascia ch'io pianga' (from Rinaldo) and #10 'Ombra mai fu' / 'Largo' (from Xerxes).

### PPT 2: Pre-Classical & The Classical Period (Slides 1–28)

#### Slides 1–2 (p. 01) — The Classical Period (1750–1820): Enlightenment Ideals, The French Revolution & Absolute Music (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `The Classical period (1750–1820) | simplicity, clarity, universality | the French Revolution (1789) | enlightenment, humanity | absolute music (vs. program music)`

PPT 2 opens with the philosophical, political, and musical transformation that defines the Classical Period (1750–1820) — the era of Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven (the 'First Viennese School'), bounded by the death of J. S. Bach in 1750 and the late years of Beethoven around 1820.

Why did music change so dramatically after 1750? Look at the historical keywords on Slide 2: 'Enlightenment, humanity' and 'the French Revolution (1789)'. During the 18th-century Age of Enlightenment, philosophers (Voltaire, Rousseau, Kant) championed reason, naturalness, individual human dignity, and universal human rights over feudal aristocratic privilege and religious dogma — culminating politically in the American Revolution (1776) and the French Revolution (1789, 'Liberty, Equality, Fraternity').

In music, this Enlightenment worldview rejected the dense, complicated, elitist polyphony and heavy ornamentation of the late Baroque era. Instead, Classical composers pursued three core ideals listed at the top of Slide 2: SIMPLICITY, CLARITY, and UNIVERSALITY! Music should have clear, singable, balanced melodies supported by transparent homophonic accompaniment (such as the Alberti bass) so that any listener — not just a royal courtier or church scholar, but the rising middle-class public in Vienna, London, and Paris — could immediately follow and enjoy it.

Finally, Prof. Chae introduces a critical aesthetic contrast on Slide 2: **Absolute Music (vs. Program Music)**. What is Absolute Music? It is purely instrumental music (such as a Classical Symphony, String Quartet, Piano Sonata, or Concerto) that has NO external story, poem, picture, or descriptive title — its meaning lies 100% inside its own musical structure, proportions, and formal logic (like Sonata Form). By contrast, Program Music (such as Vivaldi's 'Four Seasons' in the Baroque era, Beethoven's Symphony No. 6 'Pastoral', or 19th-century Romantic symphonic poems) attempts to depict an extra-musical narrative, scene, or story in sound.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Simplicity, Clarity & Universality**: The three aesthetic pillars of the Classical period (1750–1820): clear, balanced phrasing and transparent homophonic texture accessible to all listeners.
- **The Age of Enlightenment & The French Revolution (1789)**: Philosophical and political movements emphasizing human reason, naturalness, equality, and humanity; fueled the rise of middle-class public concerts and human-centered operas.
- **Absolute Music vs. Program Music**: Absolute Music = instrumental music written purely for its own formal/structural beauty with no external story (e.g., Symphony No. 40, Piano Sonata K. 545) | Program Music = instrumental music that depicts a specific non-musical story, poem, or nature scene (e.g., Vivaldi's Four Seasons).

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Memorize every keyword on Slide 2: The Classical Period (1750–1820) = Simplicity, Clarity, Universality | The French Revolution (1789) | Enlightenment & Humanity | Absolute Music (vs. Program Music).

#### Slide 3 (p. 02) — Pre-Classical Transition Styles (Rococo / Galant vs. Empfindsamer Stil) & J. S. Bach's Three Sons (Pre-Classical (c. 1730–1770))
- **On the Slide**: `Pre-classical music | Rococo; Galant style; empfindsamer stil (sturm und drang) | J. S. Bach’s sons: Wilhelm Friedemann Bach, Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach, Johann Christian Bach | C. P. E. Bach – empfindsamer stil (Solfeggietto, Fantasia for Clavichord in C Minor) | J. C. Bach – galant style; W. A. Mozart (Sonata for 2 pianos)`

Music did not jump overnight from J. S. Bach's dense Baroque fugues to Mozart's Classical symphonies. Between roughly 1730 and 1770 lay the Pre-Classical transition period, and ironically, the pioneers who rebelled against Baroque polyphony were J. S. Bach's own sons: Wilhelm Friedemann Bach (W. F. Bach), Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach (C. P. E. Bach), and Johann Christian Bach (J. C. Bach)!

On Slide 3, Prof. Chae contrasts the two competing Pre-Classical styles and matches each style to a specific son of J. S. Bach — a guaranteed exam topic:

1. Rococo / Galant Style ('Style galant' — French/Italian courtly elegance) → Johann Christian Bach (J. C. Bach, 1735–1782): Just as French Rococo interior design replaced heavy Baroque gold columns with light, pastel, graceful curves, the musical Galant Style replaced complex polyphony with light, charming, elegant, easy-to-follow melodies over simple chordal accompaniment. J. S. Bach's youngest son, Johann Christian Bach (the 'London Bach'), moved to Italy and then London, becoming the master of the Galant style. When the 8-year-old child prodigy Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart visited London in 1764, J. C. Bach befriended and mentored him — making J. C. Bach and the Galant style a decisive direct influence on Mozart!

2. Empfindsamer Stil ('Sensitive Style' — North German emotional expressiveness) & Sturm und Drang ('Storm and Stress') → Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach (C. P. E. Bach, 1714–1788): In Northern Germany (Berlin and Hamburg), J. S. Bach's second son, C. P. E. Bach, felt that merely being 'polite and charming' (Galant) was too shallow. He pioneered the Empfindsamer Stil ('Sensitive Style'), characterized by sudden shifts in mood, unexpected harmonic surprises, dramatic pauses, nervous rhythmic agitation, and intimate emotional sighs (especially on his favorite instrument, the touch-sensitive Clavichord, and in his famous keyboard piece 'Solfeggietto'). In the 1760s–1770s, this intensified into Sturm und Drang ('Storm and Stress', named after a German literary movement), using turbulent minor keys, tremolos, and syncopations — directly influencing Haydn, Mozart's two minor-key symphonies, and Beethoven!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Rococo / Galant Style**: Light, graceful, aristocratic, homophonic Pre-Classical style emphasizing pleasing melody and elegance over complex counterpoint.
- **Johann Christian Bach (J. C. Bach, 'London Bach')**: J. S. Bach's youngest son; champion of the Galant style who directly mentored and influenced the young W. A. Mozart.
- **Empfindsamer Stil ('Sensitive Style') & Sturm und Drang ('Storm and Stress')**: North German Pre-Classical style featuring sudden dynamic/emotional contrasts, harmonic surprises, and minor-key tension.
- **Carl Philipp Emanuel Bach (C. P. E. Bach, 'Berlin/Hamburg Bach')**: J. S. Bach's second son; master of the Empfindsamer Stil ('Solfeggietto', 'Fantasia in C Minor'); deeply admired by Haydn, Mozart, and Beethoven.

#### Slide 4 (p. 03) — Classical Keyboard Music: Sonatinas (Clementi, Kuhlau) & Mozart's K. 545 (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Keyboard music: fortepiano | M. Clementi, J. Dussek, A. Diabelli, F. Kuhlau, J. N. Hummel, etc. | Clementi op. 36 no. 1 (1st & 3rd mvt.) | Clementi Op. 36 no. 3 | Kuhlau op. 20 no. 1 | Mozart K. 545`

As the newly invented piano (fortepiano) replaced the harpsichord in middle-class homes across Europe, a huge market emerged for domestic keyboard music and pedagogical pieces: the Classical Piano Sonata and the Sonatina (literally a 'little sonata' — shorter, lighter, and technically simpler than a full sonata, with a brief development section). On Slide 4, Prof. Chae lists the leading Classical keyboard composers — Muzio Clementi, Jan Ladislav Dussek, Anton Diabelli, Friedrich Kuhlau, Johann Nepomuk Hummel, and W. A. Mozart — and places THREE of their C-Major works on your 49-track listening list (#22 Clementi, #23 Kuhlau, and #40 Mozart K. 545)!

Because ALL THREE (#22, #23, and #40) are bright, cheerful Classical solo piano pieces in C Major that use an Alberti bass (a broken-chord left-hand accompaniment pattern: low–high–middle–high, C–G–E–G), they form the single biggest sound-alike trap on the listening exam! Here is how to tell them apart in the first 3 seconds:

• Track #22 — Muzio Clementi (1752–1832): Piano Sonatina in C Major, Op. 36 No. 1 (1st Mvt, Allegro): Opens immediately with a crisp, bouncy, detached broken C-major triad in the right hand ('C–E–G–C [high]–G–G!') over simple quarter-note octaves in the left hand.

• Track #23 — Friedrich Kuhlau (1786–1832): Piano Sonatina in C Major, Op. 20 No. 1 (1st Mvt, Allegro): Opens with a smooth, connected (legato), lyrical singing melody over a gentle, rolling 8th-note Alberti bass — much more flowing and songful than Clementi's staccato bounce.

• Track #40 — W. A. Mozart (1756–1791): Piano Sonata No. 16 in C Major, K. 545 ('Sonata facile', 1st Mvt, Allegro): Opens with the iconic long-held melody note C followed by a rising step E–G ('C— E–G–B–C–D–C!') over a brisk, ticking 16th-note Alberti bass, quickly breaking into sparkling rapid scale runs.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Sonatina vs. Sonata**: A Sonatina ('little sonata') is a shorter, lighter, technically accessible multi-movement keyboard work in sonata form, championed by Clementi (Op. 36) and Kuhlau (Op. 20).
- **Alberti Bass**: The signature Classical keyboard left-hand accompaniment pattern: instead of playing a block chord all at once, the left hand plays the notes of the chord in a steady repeating broken pattern (lowest – highest – middle – highest), keeping the texture light and clear.
- **The C-Major Keyboard Trio (#22 Clementi, #23 Kuhlau, #40 Mozart)**: #22 Clementi Op. 36 No. 1 = bouncy staccato arpeggio jump | #23 Kuhlau Op. 20 No. 1 = smooth legato singing curve over gentle 8th-note Alberti bass | #40 Mozart K. 545 = brisk 16th-note Alberti bass + sparkling rapid scale runs.

#### Slide 5 (p. 04) — Invention of the Piano: Bartolomeo Cristofori (c. 1709, Italy) — Fortepiano / Pianoforte (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Fortepiano (= pianoforte = piano): 1709~ Cristofori, Italy | Amadeus clip | Für Elise`

Slide 5 contains one of Prof. Chae's favorite factual theory questions: Who invented the piano, when, where, and why was it named 'Fortepiano' (or 'Pianoforte')?

Around 1709 in Florence, Italy, an instrument maker named **Bartolomeo Cristofori** (working for the Medici court) solved the greatest limitation of the Baroque harpsichord. Remember from PPT 1 Slide 23 that a harpsichord plucks its strings with quills, so pressing a key harder or softer makes zero difference to the volume. Cristofori invented an ingenious escapement mechanism where pressing a key throws a small leather-covered (later felt-covered) **hammer** that STRIKES the string and immediately rebounds!

Cristofori called his invention *'gravicembalo col piano e forte'* — literally a 'harpsichord with soft and loud' — which was shortened to **Fortepiano** or **Pianoforte** (and finally **Piano**). Because the hammers strike the strings with the exact velocity of the player's finger, a performer could now play soft (*piano*), loud (*forte*), sudden accents (*sfz*), and smooth gradual *crescendos* and *decrescendos* on a single keyboard! (In modern usage, historians often use the word 'fortepiano' to refer specifically to the lighter, wooden-framed 18th-century Classical instrument played by Mozart, Haydn, and early Beethoven — as seen in the 'Amadeus' film clip on this slide — and 'piano' for the heavier iron-framed 19th–20th-century instrument, though on Slide 5 Prof. Chae notes `Fortepiano (= pianoforte = piano)` as equivalent names for Cristofori's invention.)

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Bartolomeo Cristofori (c. 1709, Italy)**: Italian instrument maker who invented the piano around 1709 in Florence by replacing the harpsichord's plucking quills with rebounding hammers.
- **Fortepiano = Pianoforte = Piano ('Loud-Soft')**: Named after the Italian words 'forte' (loud) and 'piano' (soft) because hammer action allows the player to vary dynamic volume continuously through finger pressure.

#### Slide 6 (p. 05) — Gluck's Opera Reformation («Orfeo ed Euridice») & Sonata Form (Sonata-Allegro Structure) (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Opera | C. W. Gluck: opera reformation (vs. Baroque opera) «Orfeo ed Euridice» | sonata form (Mozart Symphony No. 40, Parasite)`

Slide 6 combines two monumental pillars of the Classical period: (1) Christoph Willibald Gluck's Opera Reformation, and (2) Sonata Form (Sonata-Allegro Form).

1. C. W. Gluck (1714–1787) & The Opera Reformation (vs. Baroque Opera): By the mid-18th century, Baroque Italian serious opera (*opera seria*) had degenerated into a vain circus for spoiled castrato superstars. Plots were ridiculous, recitatives were dry and boring, and during *da capo* arias singers stopped the drama completely to improvise endless, showy vocal gymnastics just to show off their high notes. Gluck launched his famous **Opera Reformation** (most famously in his 1762 reform opera **«Orfeo ed Euridice»**, featuring the famous aria *'Che farò senza Euridice?'*) to bring Enlightenment simplicity and dramatic truth back to opera! Gluck declared that **music must serve the poetry and drama** — he stripped away vain vocal ornamentation, made the orchestra and chorus integral parts of the dramatic action, and replaced unnatural artificiality with noble simplicity.

2. Sonata Form (also called Sonata-Allegro Form): Do NOT confuse 'Sonata' (a 3- or 4-movement genre) with **'Sonata Form'** (the specific musical blueprint used inside the **1st movement** — and often the 4th movement — of almost every Classical Symphony, Sonata, and String Quartet!). Prof. Chae illustrates Sonata Form using the 1st movement of Mozart's **Symphony No. 40 in G Minor, K. 550** (Listening Track #31). How does Sonata Form work? It is a dramatic three-act musical journey:

• **Act 1 — Exposition**: Introduces the main characters! First comes **Theme 1** (energetic, dramatic, in the home/tonic key, e.g., G minor in Mozart's 40th), followed by a modulating bridge to **Theme 2** (lyrical, contrasting, in a new key, e.g., B-flat major), and a closing theme. (In Classical performances, the entire Exposition is repeated so the audience memorizes both themes.)

• **Act 2 — Development**: The conflict and adventure! The composer takes fragments (motives) of Theme 1 and Theme 2 and breaks them apart, combines them in counterpoint, and modulates rapidly through restless, unstable keys, building intense harmonic tension.

• **Act 3 — Recapitulation (+ Coda)**: Resolution and homecoming! Theme 1 returns in the home (tonic) key, and now **Theme 2 ALSO returns in the home key**, resolving the tonal conflict, rounded off by a concluding tail section called the **Coda**.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Christoph Willibald Gluck (1714–1787) & Opera Reformation**: Reformed opera against the vanity and artificial ornamentation of Baroque opera seria; in «Orfeo ed Euridice» (1762), Gluck insisted on naturalness, simplicity, and making music serve the dramatic story.
- **Sonata Form (Exposition → Development → Recapitulation + Coda)**: The 3-part structural blueprint of a Classical 1st movement: (1) Exposition (Theme 1 in tonic + Theme 2 in contrasting key) → (2) Development (harmonic tension, fragmentation, modulation) → (3) Recapitulation (both themes return in the home key) + Coda.
- **Mozart: Symphony No. 40 in G Minor, K. 550 — 1st Mvt (Track #31)**: Textbook example of Sonata Form; opens with restless pulsing violas and the urgent 3-note half-step sighing motif ('da-da-da, da-da-da, da-da-da—DUM!').

#### Slide 7 (p. 06) — Classical Instrumental Genres & Forms (Part 1): Symphony, Sonata & Rondo Form (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Symphony (Beethoven No. 5, 4th mvt. | Orchestra instruments & seating position) | Sonata (Beethoven Violin Sonata No. 5 'Spring') | Rondo (Mozart 'Rondo alla Turca')`

Slides 7 and 8 define the six major instrumental genres and forms of the Classical period: Symphony, Sonata, Rondo, String Quartet, Concerto, and Theme & Variations. On Slide 7, Prof. Chae explains the first three:

1. Symphony: An ambitious, large-scale work for full symphony orchestra (divided into four instrumental families: Strings [violins I & II, violas, cellos, double basses], Woodwinds [flutes, oboes, clarinets, bassoons], Brass [French horns, trumpets, and in Beethoven's 5th/9th, trombones], and Percussion [timpani]). A standard Classical Symphony has **4 movements**: (1st Mvt) Fast & dramatic in Sonata Form; (2nd Mvt) Slow & lyrical (Andante or Adagio); (3rd Mvt) Dance-inspired **Minuet and Trio** (or in Beethoven, a fast **Scherzo**) in triple 3/4 meter; and (4th Mvt) Fast, brilliant Finale in Sonata or Rondo form (illustrated here by Beethoven's triumphant C-major 4th movement of Symphony No. 5, Track #44!).

2. Classical Sonata: A multi-movement work (usually 3 or 4 movements) either for a solo keyboard instrument (Piano Sonata, like Mozart's K. 545 #40 or Beethoven's Pathétique #46 / Appassionata #47) OR for one solo melody instrument + piano working as equals (such as Beethoven's radiant Violin Sonata No. 5 in F Major, Op. 24 'Spring', Track #45!).

3. Rondo Form (`A–B–A–C–A` or `A–B–A–C–A–B–A`): A lively, symmetrical musical form where a catchy main theme (**Refrain 'A'**, always in the home key) keeps coming back in alternation with contrasting middle sections (**Episodes 'B', 'C'**, etc.). Because Rondo form is tuneful, energetic, and easy to follow, Classical composers loved using it for the final movement (3rd or 4th movement) of sonatas, concertos, and symphonies — illustrated by Mozart's famous 'Rondo alla Turca' ('Turkish March', 3rd mvt of Piano Sonata No. 11, K. 331, Track #41)!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Symphony (4-Movement Orchestral Cycle)**: 4 movements for full orchestra (Strings, Woodwinds, Brass, Timpani): 1. Fast (Sonata form) — 2. Slow — 3. Minuet/Scherzo (3/4) — 4. Fast Finale (Sonata or Rondo).
- **Classical Sonata**: 3- or 4-movement chamber work for solo piano OR for one instrument (e.g., violin or cello) + piano (e.g., Beethoven's Violin Sonata No. 5 'Spring', Track #45).
- **Rondo Form (A–B–A–C–A)**: Form featuring a recurring main theme (Refrain 'A') alternating with contrasting episodes ('B', 'C'), exemplified by Mozart's 'Rondo alla Turca' (Track #41) and Beethoven's 'Für Elise'.

#### Slide 8 (p. 07) — Classical Instrumental Genres & Forms (Part 2): String Quartet, Solo Concerto (Cadenza) & Theme and Variations (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `String quartet: chamber ensemble, 2 vn. 1 vl. 1 vc. (Haydn 'The Lark') | Concerto (Haydn Cello in C, 7’30’’ cadenza | Haydn Trumpet) | Variations ('Ah, vous dirai-je, Maman')`

Slide 8 completes the survey of Classical genres and forms with three more heavily tested topics — and all four videos embedded on Slide 8 are on your official 49-track listening list (#26, #28, #29, #30)!

1. String Quartet (Chamber Music): The most prestigious chamber-music genre of the Classical era (perfected by its father, Joseph Haydn). Memorize the exact 4-instrument lineup written on Slide 8: **2 Violins (Violin I & Violin II) + 1 Viola + 1 Cello (`2 vn., 1 vl., 1 vc.`)** — NO double bass, NO piano, and NO conductor! Like a mini-symphony for four friends conversing in a room, a String Quartet has 4 movements (Fast Sonata – Slow – Minuet – Fast Finale), illustrated by Haydn's String Quartet Op. 64 No. 5 'The Lark' (Track #26).

2. Classical Solo Concerto & The Cadenza: While the Baroque era loved the Concerto Grosso (a small group of soloists), the Classical era favored the **Solo Concerto** — a **3-movement work (`1. Fast – 2. Slow – 3. Fast`)** pitting ONE star soloist (piano, violin, cello, clarinet, or trumpet) against the orchestra. Notice that a Concerto has **3 movements** (it omits the Minuet movement of the 4-movement Symphony!). Near the end of the 1st movement (and sometimes the 3rd), the entire orchestra suddenly stops on a suspenseful chord and remains completely silent while the soloist plays a dazzling, unaccompanied virtuosic solo showcase called a **Cadenza** (historically improvised on the spot by the soloist, ending with a long trill to signal the orchestra to re-enter!). Prof. Chae specifically marks `7'30'' cadenza` on Han-Na Chang's performance of Haydn's Cello Concerto No. 1 in C Major (Track #28), alongside Haydn's Trumpet Concerto in E-flat Major (Track #29).

3. Theme and Variations (`A, A1, A2, A3, A4...`): An independent form (or slow-movement form inside a symphony/quartet) where a simple, memorable melody (**Theme 'A'**) is presented first and then repeated through a series of transformations (**Variations**), altering the rhythm, accompaniment speed, harmony (including a minor-key variation), or counterpoint while keeping the underlying harmonic outline recognizable — illustrated by Mozart's 12 Variations on *'Ah, vous dirai-je, Maman'*, K. 265 ('Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star', Track #30)!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **String Quartet (`2 vn., 1 vl., 1 vc.`)**: 4-movement chamber work for exactly 4 string instruments: 2 Violins (1st & 2nd) + 1 Viola + 1 Cello. No piano, no double bass, no conductor!
- **Classical Solo Concerto & Cadenza**: 3-movement work (Fast–Slow–Fast) for 1 soloist + orchestra. Features an unaccompanied virtuosic solo display called a Cadenza near the end of the movement.
- **Theme and Variations (`A, A1, A2, A3...`)**: Form in which a main theme is stated and then repeated with progressive melodic, rhythmic, and harmonic decorations (e.g., Track #24 Haydn 'Surprise' 2nd mvt, Track #27 Haydn 'Emperor' 2nd mvt, Track #30 Mozart 'Twinkle' Variations).

#### Slide 9 (p. 08) — Franz Joseph Haydn (1732–1809, Austria): Prince Esterházy's Court & The Hoboken Catalogue (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Franz Joseph Haydn (1732–1809, Austria) | Prince Esterházy | Hoboken (Hob.) | symphony, string quartet, oratorio, sonata, concerto`

Slides 9 through 12 cover the eldest master of the First Viennese School: **Franz Joseph Haydn (1732–1809, Austria)** — affectionately called 'Papa Haydn' because he was the beloved mentor to Mozart, the teacher of young Beethoven, and the **'Father of the Symphony'** and **'Father of the String Quartet'**.

Look at the first keyword on Slide 9: **Prince Esterházy**. Unlike Mozart (who broke away from his patron to freelance) and Beethoven (who was an independent freelancer), Haydn spent nearly 30 years (1761–1790) working happily as full-time Kapellmeister (Director of Music) for the fabulously wealthy Hungarian noble **Esterházy family** (especially Prince Nikolaus Esterházy) at their lavish Esterháza Palace ('the Hungarian Versailles'). Wearing a court uniform (livery), Haydn managed the palace orchestra, composed symphonies, string quartets, and operas on demand, and famously remarked: *'There was no one near to confuse me, so I was forced to become original!'* Only after Prince Nikolaus died in 1790 did Haydn travel to London as an international celebrity (composing his famous 12 'London Symphonies', Nos. 93–104, including 'Surprise' #94 and 'The Clock' #101).

Second, remember Haydn's catalogue system: **Hoboken (abbreviated `Hob.`)**, compiled by Dutch musicologist Anthony van Hoboken, which groups Haydn's vast output by genre (e.g., `Hob. I` = Symphonies, `Hob. III` = String Quartets, `Hob. XVI` = Keyboard Sonatas).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Prince Esterházy (Patronage System)**: Wealthy Hungarian noble family that employed Haydn as court Kapellmeister for ~30 years, providing him with his own resident orchestra.
- **Hoboken Catalogue (`Hob.`)**: Thematic catalogue of Franz Joseph Haydn's works (compiled by Anthony van Hoboken).
- **'Father of the Symphony' & 'Father of the String Quartet'**: Haydn standardized the 4-movement Classical structure of both the Symphony (106 works) and the String Quartet (68 works).

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**

#### Slide 10 (p. 09) — Haydn's 106 Symphonies: 'Surprise' (#94), 'Farewell' (#45) & 'The Clock' (#101) (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Symphony: 106 works | Surprise (No. 94) | Farewell (No. 45) | The Clock (No. 101, 8’ 20’’)`

Haydn composed an astonishing **106 Symphonies** (traditionally numbered 1–104 plus 'Symphony A' and 'Symphony B'), transforming the symphony from a brief 10-minute court overture into the grand 30-minute centerpiece of the concert hall. On Slide 10, Prof. Chae highlights three nicknamed Haydn symphonies — every single one of which has a famous story that she loves testing on the exam:

1. **Symphony No. 94 in G Major, 'Surprise'** (2nd Mvt *Andante*, Listening Track #24): Composed in 1791 for his London public concert series. Why is it called 'Surprise'? The 2nd movement opens with a childlike, tiptoeing staccato C-major theme (`C–C–E–E–G–G–E`), repeated even more softly (`pp`) as if lulling well-fed London concertgoers to sleep after dinner — and then, at `0:32`, the entire orchestra + timpani slams a **sudden, deafening fortissimo (`ff`) chord**! Haydn joked, *'That will make the ladies jump!'* before proceeding into a set of charming Theme and Variations.

2. **Symphony No. 45 in F-sharp Minor, 'Farewell'** (1772): Why is it called 'Farewell'? In 1772, Prince Nikolaus Esterházy kept his court orchestra trapped at his remote summer palace at Esterháza far longer into the autumn than planned, and the homesick musicians begged Haydn to help them return to their wives and children in Eisenstadt. Instead of writing a petition, Haydn composed Symphony No. 45: in the final *Adagio*, one instrument after another finishes its part, **blows out its music-stand candle, picks up its instrument, and walks off the stage** until only two muted violins are left playing softly! Prince Esterházy got the hint immediately and announced the next morning, *'Since they all have left, we might as well go too!'*

3. **Symphony No. 101 in D Major, 'The Clock'** (2nd Mvt *Andante*, Listening Track #25, cued at `8:27~`): Another London symphony (1794), nicknamed 'The Clock' because the 2nd movement (starting at `8:27` in your exam video) opens with a steady, mechanical **'tick-tock, tick-tock'** 8th-note accompaniment in the bassoons and pizzicato strings underneath a graceful violin melody.

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **106 Symphonies**: Total number of symphonies composed by Franz Joseph Haydn.
- **Symphony No. 94 'Surprise' — 2nd Mvt (Track #24)**: Tiptoeing soft (`p` → `pp`) C-major theme interrupted by a sudden explosive `ff` timpani/orchestra chord, followed by variations.
- **Symphony No. 45 'Farewell' (Theory Story)**: In the finale, musicians blow out their candles and leave the stage one by one as a polite hint to Prince Esterházy to let them go home to their families.
- **Symphony No. 101 'The Clock' — 2nd Mvt (Track #25, 8:27~)**: Named after the steady 'tick-tock' pulse played by bassoons and plucked lower strings starting at 8:27.

#### Slide 13 (p. 12) — Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791, Austria): Child Prodigy, Influences, Freelance Independence & Köchel Catalogue (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791, Austria) | child prodigy | influences -> galant style, empfindsamer stil, Italian music (J. C. Bach), F. J. Haydn (form) | opera buffa; clarity, balance; counterpoint; independent; social standing | Köchel catalogue (K.)`

Slides 13 through 20 (8 full slides!) are devoted to **Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756–1791, Austria)** — arguably the most universal musical genius in Western history, who composed over 600 masterpieces across every single genre before dying in Vienna at just **35 years old**. Let's unpack every single keyword on Slide 13:

1. **Child Prodigy**: Born in Salzburg, Austria, trained by his violinist-composer father Leopold Mozart. By age 4 he played keyboard effortlessly; by age 5 he was composing; and between ages 6 and 15 his father took him and his sister Nannerl on grand tours across the royal courts of Europe (Munich, Vienna, Paris, London, Rome), where young Wolfgang sight-read anything, improvised blindfolded, and wrote down Allegri's secret Vatican *Miserere* from memory after hearing it once!

2. **Four Musical Influences Synthesized by Mozart**: Because he toured all of Europe as a child, Mozart absorbed every national style and fused them into Classical perfection: (a) the graceful, melodious **Galant Style** and **Italian music** learned directly from **Johann Christian Bach (J. C. Bach)** in London; (b) the dramatic emotional shifts of the North German **Empfindsamer Stil** (C. P. E. Bach); (c) the structural mastery of **Sonata Form, Symphony, and String Quartet** learned from his close friend and mentor **Franz Joseph Haydn** in Vienna (to whom Mozart dedicated his six 'Haydn Quartets'); and (d) late in life, the rich **Counterpoint** of J. S. Bach and Handel!

3. **Independent Composer & Social Standing**: Unlike Haydn (who worked contentedly as a costumed palace servant for Prince Esterházy), Mozart despised being treated like a valet by his tyrannical employer, Prince-Archbishop Colloredo of Salzburg. In **1781**, Mozart broke away (literally kicked out of the Archbishop's service!) and moved to **Vienna** to live as one of history's first **independent freelance composers** — earning his living through public subscription concerts, piano teaching, publishing, and opera commissions.

4. **Köchel Catalogue (`K.` or `KV`)**: In 1862, Austrian botanist and musicologist Ludwig von Köchel catalogued all of Mozart's works in chronological order from **K. 1** (a keyboard piece written at age 5) to **K. 626** (his unfinished *Requiem in D Minor*, left on his deathbed in 1791).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Mozart's 4 Key Influences (Slide 13)**: 1. Galant style & Italian music (via J. C. Bach) | 2. Empfindsamer Stil | 3. Classical Form (via F. J. Haydn) | 4. Baroque Counterpoint (J. S. Bach).
- **Independent Freelance Life (Social Standing)**: Rebelled against the feudal patronage system in Salzburg (1781) to work as an independent composer and pianist in Vienna.
- **Köchel Catalogue (`K.` / `KV`)**: Chronological catalogue of Mozart's 626+ works compiled by Ludwig von Köchel (K. 1 to K. 626 Requiem).

**Midterm Exam Takeaways:**
- Memorize Slide 13's exact influence chain for Mozart: J. C. Bach (Galant style & Italian music) + Empfindsamer Stil + F. J. Haydn (Form) + Opera buffa + Clarity, Balance & Counterpoint + Independent social standing + Köchel (`K.`).

#### Slide 14 (p. 13) — Mozart's 41 Symphonies (No. 25, No. 40, No. 41 'Jupiter') & 18 Piano Sonatas (K. 310, K. 331, K. 545) (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Symphony: 41 symphonies | No. 41, K. 551 in C major, 'Jupiter', 4th mvt., fuga, 36’ | No. 25, K. 183 in G minor, Amadeus opening, 2’ | Sonata: 18 piano sonatas | K. 310 in A minor | K. 331 in A major, 18’50’’ Rondo alla Turca`

Slide 14 covers Mozart's **41 Symphonies** and **18 Piano Sonatas** — and every single work listed on this slide is high-yield exam material:

1. **Mozart's 41 Symphonies & The 'Two Minor Symphonies' Rule**: Out of all 41 numbered symphonies Mozart composed, **39 are in bright Major keys** and **ONLY TWO are in a Minor key** — and both of those minor-key symphonies are in **G Minor** and both are on your exam listening list (#31 and #32)!

• **Symphony No. 25 in G Minor, K. 183 ('Little G Minor', Listening Track #32)**: Composed in 1773 when Mozart was just **17 years old** under the influence of the turbulent *Sturm und Drang* ('Storm and Stress') style. Famous as the dramatic opening soundtrack of the 1984 movie ***Amadeus***! How do you tell No. 25 (#32) apart from No. 40 (#31) on the listening test? **No. 25 (#32)** opens immediately with tense, syncopated string leaps + long, wailing sustained notes in the oboes and horns; **No. 40 (#31)** opens with soft, rapid pulsing violas and the hurried 3-note half-step sighing melody (*'da-da-da, da-da-da, da-da-da—DUM!'*).

• **Symphony No. 41 in C Major, K. 551 ('Jupiter', 1788)**: Mozart's final, grandest symphony! Why does Prof. Chae write `'4th mvt., fuga, 36’'` next to Symphony No. 41? Because in the triumphant 4th-movement Finale of the 'Jupiter' Symphony, Mozart fuses Classical Sonata Form with a dazzling **5-voice Baroque Fugue (fuga)** where five different themes are played simultaneously in invertible counterpoint!

2. **Mozart's 18 Piano Sonatas**: In addition to Piano Sonata No. 16 in C Major, K. 545 (Track #40 from Slide 4), Slide 14 highlights two contrasting piano sonatas on your listening list:

• **Piano Sonata No. 8 in A Minor, K. 310 (1st Mvt *Allegro maestoso*, Listening Track #42)**: Composed in Paris in the summer of **1778** right after Mozart's beloved mother suddenly fell ill and died while traveling with him. One of only two minor-key piano sonatas Mozart ever wrote, it opens with a fierce, tragic, march-like dotted melody in the right hand over a relentless, pounding, drum-like A-minor chord pulse in the left hand.

• **Piano Sonata No. 11 in A Major, K. 331 (3rd Mvt *'Rondo alla Turca'* / 'Turkish March', Listening Track #41)**: Composed c. 1783; an unusual sonata because none of its three movements is in Sonata Form (1st mvt = Theme & Variations; 2nd mvt = Minuet; 3rd mvt = *Rondo alla Turca*). In 18th-century Vienna, audiences were fascinated by the Ottoman Turkish military marching bands (*Janissary bands*) with their bass drums, cymbals, and triangles — which Mozart imitates on the piano with rolled left-hand grace-note chords and jaunty A-minor → A-major march refrains!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **41 Symphonies & Only 2 in Minor (Both in G Minor!)**: Mozart wrote 41 numbered symphonies. #### Slide 15 (p. 14) — Mozart's Concertos: 23 Piano Concertos (K. 467, K. 466), Clarinet Concerto (K. 622) & Violin Concerto No. 5 (K. 219) (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Concerto: 23 piano concertos | K. 467 no. 21 in C major, 'Elvira Madigan' | K. 466 no. 20 in D minor | clarinet concerto K. 622 in A major ('Out of Africa') | violin concerto no. 5, K. 219 in A major (Bomsori Kim)`

When Mozart moved to Vienna in 1781 as an independent freelance composer-pianist, his main source of income was organizing his own subscription concerts ('academies'), where he premiered his **23 Piano Concertos** from the keyboard (often conducting the orchestra while playing the solo piano part!). On Slide 15, Prof. Chae covers four essential Mozart concertos, three of which are on your 49-track listening list (#36, #39, #43):

1. **Piano Concerto No. 21 in C Major, K. 467 — 2nd Mvt (*Andante*, Listening Track #36, nicknamed 'Elvira Madigan')**: Composed in 1785; nicknamed 'Elvira Madigan' after the 1967 Swedish film that used this dreamy F-major slow movement as its love theme. How do you identify Track #36 instantly? Listen for the hypnotic **3-layer string texture** at the very opening: (1) steady plucked *pizzicato* arpeggios in the cellos/basses, (2) soft pulsing triplet chords in the middle strings, and (3) a floating, muted violin melody on top — before the solo piano enters gently at `1:22`!

2. **Piano Concerto No. 20 in D Minor, K. 466 (1785)**: One of only two minor-key piano concertos Mozart wrote (along with No. 24 in C minor, K. 491); so dark, stormy, and operatic (resembling *Don Giovanni*) that it became Ludwig van Beethoven's favorite Mozart concerto (Beethoven even wrote his own solo cadenzas for it, played here by Korean pianist Yeol Eum Son!).

3. **Clarinet Concerto in A Major, K. 622 — 2nd Mvt (*Adagio*, Listening Track #43)**: Mozart's **final purely instrumental masterpiece**, completed in October 1791 just two months before his death for his close friend and fellow Freemason, the virtuoso clarinetist **Anton Stadler**. Because the **Clarinet** was a newly perfected woodwind instrument in the late 18th century (capable of silky *legato* and a vast three-octave dynamic range from whisper-soft to warm full tone), Mozart made it the quintessential Classical woodwind voice. In the 2nd movement (*Adagio*, featured in the movie ***Out of Africa***), the solo clarinet sings an aching, autumnal D-major aria answered tenderly by the orchestra.

4. **Violin Concerto No. 5 in A Major, K. 219 ('Turkish') — 1st Mvt (*Allegro aperto*, Listening Track #39)**: Composed in Salzburg in 1775 when Mozart was **19 years old** (the grandest of his 5 authentic violin concertos). It has a completely unique **'false-start' surprise** on the listening test: after a bright, courtly orchestral opening (`0:00–1:28`), the solo violin enters at `1:29` NOT at the fast tempo, but in a **dreamy, suspended slow *Adagio* introduction** floating over shimmering strings, before suddenly snapping back into the lively *Allegro aperto* main theme at `2:18`! (It is nicknamed 'Turkish' because its 3rd-movement Rondo finale bursts into a wild minor-key Turkish Janissary episode where the cellos and basses slap their strings with the wood of their bows — *col legno*!)

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **23 Piano Concertos (K. 467 & K. 466)**: Mozart composed 23 original piano concertos: No. 21 in C Major, K. 467 (Track #36, 2nd Mvt Andante 'Elvira Madigan', muted violins over triplet + pizzicato strings) and No. 20 in D Minor, K. 466 (dark minor concerto admired by Beethoven).
- **Clarinet Concerto in A Major, K. 622 — 2nd Mvt Adagio (Track #43)**: Mozart's final instrumental work (1791), written for clarinetist Anton Stadler; its warm, woody solo clarinet Adagio was immortalized in the film 'Out of Africa'.
- **Violin Concerto No. 5 in A Major, K. 219 'Turkish' — 1st Mvt (Track #39)**: Written at age 19; the solo violin enters with a surprise slow Adagio episode before launching into the bright Allegro aperto main theme.

#### Slide 16 (p. 15) — Mozart's Serenades, Divertimentos & Sacred Motet: «Eine kleine Nachtmusik» (K. 525) & «Exsultate, jubilate» (Classical (1750–1820))

In 18th-century Vienna and Salzburg, aristocrats and wealthy citizens frequently hired ensembles to play light, festive outdoor evening music for garden parties, weddings, and social gatherings — genres called the **Serenade** (from *sera* = evening) and the **Divertimento** (from *divertire* = to amuse/entertain). On Slide 16, Prof. Chae highlights three works:

1. **«Eine kleine Nachtmusik» (Serenade No. 13 in G Major for Strings, K. 525 — 1st Mvt *Allegro*, Listening Track #33)**: Literally meaning *'A Little Night Music'* (composed in Vienna in 1787). In the famous opening scene of the movie *Amadeus* shown on this slide, the elderly composer Antonio Salieri plays the opening notes on the piano for a young priest — the priest doesn't recognize any of Salieri's own music, but the instant Salieri plays the rocket-like G-major unison arpeggio of Mozart's *Eine kleine Nachtmusik* (`G–D–G–D–G–B–D!`), the priest lights up and hums along! That explosive unison string rocket is your 1-second ID for Track #33.

2. **Divertimento in D Major, K. 136**: Composed when Mozart was 16 in Salzburg; sparkling 3-movement chamber entertainment for strings.

3. **«Exsultate, jubilate», K. 165 — 'Alleluja'**: A brilliant sacred Latin solo **Motet** composed in Milan in 1773 (originally for castrato Venanzio Rauzzini, today a signature showcase for coloratura sopranos like Cecilia Bartoli and Korean soprano Sumi Jo), ending with a joyous, virtuosic *'Alleluja'*.

#### Slide 17 (p. 16) — Mozart's Wind Serenade ('Gran Partita') & Unfinished «Requiem in D Minor, K. 626» ('Lacrimosa' vs. 'Rex tremendae') (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Serenade for woodwinds (Amadeus — Salieri describes Mozart's music) | Requiem (K. 626): Lacrimosa (#34) | Rex tremendae (#35, 13’30’’~15’30’’)`

Slide 17 focuses on two of the most famous scenes in the film *Amadeus* and two sacred choral movements on your 49-track listening list (#34 and #35) from Mozart's final, unfinished masterpiece: the **«Requiem in D Minor, K. 626» (1791)**.

First, Prof. Chae plays the famous *Amadeus* clip where Salieri looks at the manuscript of Mozart's **Serenade No. 10 in B-flat Major for 13 Wind Instruments, K. 361 ('Gran Partita', 3rd Mvt *Adagio*)** and marvels at how a simple pulse of bassoons and basset horns becomes a transcendent oboe melody: *'Change one note and there would be diminishment; remove one phrase and the structure would fall — this was the very voice of God!'*

Second, what is the true historical story behind Mozart's **«Requiem in D Minor, K. 626»**? In July 1791, a mysterious messenger dressed in gray arrived at Mozart's Vienna apartment to anonymously commission a **Requiem Mass (Mass for the Dead)**. In the fictionalized movie *Amadeus*, that messenger is Salieri plotting Mozart's death — but in real history, the anonymous patron was **Count Franz von Walsegg**, an eccentric nobleman whose young wife had just died and who secretly wanted to pass off Mozart's Requiem as his own composition! Exhausted, feverish, and convinced he was writing the Requiem for his own funeral, the 35-year-old Mozart died on **December 5, 1791** right after sketching the **first 8 measures of the 'Lacrimosa'**! To collect the final payment so she wouldn't be left destitute, Mozart's widow Constanze had Mozart's pupil **Franz Xaver Süssmayr** complete the unfinished score.

Crucial Listening Exam Comparison — **TWO movements from Mozart's Requiem (K. 626)** are on your listening list (#34 and #35), and you must know how to tell them apart immediately:

• **Track #34 — 'Lacrimosa' (*Weeping / Tearful Day*, starts at `0:00`)**: Slowly rocking **12/8 meter** in D minor; opens with quiet, **weeping two-note violin sighs** over plucked bass before the choir enters with a rising chromatic crescendo on *'Lacrimosa dies illa'* (where Mozart died after bar 8!).

• **Track #35 — 'Rex tremendae' (*King of Tremendous Majesty*, cued at `13:30–15:30`)**: Majestic **4/4 meter** in G minor; opens with **three massive, thunderous homophonic choral shouts of *'REX!'*** separated by sharp, jagged **French-overture dotted rhythms** (*'da-da-da-DUM!'*) in the strings and trombones, before suddenly softening at the end on *'Salva me, fons pietatis'* ('Save me, fount of pity')!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **«Requiem in D Minor, K. 626» (1791)**: Mozart's final, unfinished sacred Mass for the Dead. Anonymously commissioned by Count Walsegg; left unfinished at Mozart's death (at bar 8 of the Lacrimosa) and completed by his pupil Franz Xaver Süssmayr.
- **'Lacrimosa' (Track #34) vs. 'Rex tremendae' (Track #35, 13:30~15:30)**: #34 'Lacrimosa' = slow 12/8 weeping violin sighs + rising choral crescendo | #35 'Rex tremendae' (cued at 13:30) = majestic dotted orchestral rhythms + 3 thunderous choral shouts of 'REX!' ending softly on 'Salva me'.

#### Slides 18–20 (pp. 17–19) — Mozart's Operas: Italian «Opera Buffa» (Figaro, Don Giovanni) vs. German «Singspiel» (Seraglio, Magic Flute — NO Recitativo!) (Classical (1750–1820))
- **On the Slide**: `Opera | opera reformation, opera buffa, singspiel; middle-class | Singspiel (no recitativo): «The Abduction from the Seraglio» (Finale from 2’10’’), «The Magic Flute» ('Der Hölle Rache' — Diana Damrau, Cristina Deutekom, Sumi Jo, Florence Foster Jenkins) | Opera buffa: «The Marriage of Figaro» (Overture, 'Sull'aria' in The Shawshank Redemption), «Don Giovanni»`

Slides 18, 19, and 20 cover Mozart's supreme achievements in **Classical Opera** — and contain one of the most important genre distinctions on the entire midterm: **Italian *Opera Buffa* vs. German *Singspiel***!

Why did Classical opera change from Baroque opera? Remember from PPT 1 Slide 17 and PPT 2 Slide 2 & 6 that during the Age of Enlightenment (leading up to the 1789 French Revolution), the rising **middle class** grew tired of stiff, aristocratic Baroque *opera seria* about ancient mythological gods and kings sung by castrati. Inspired by Gluck's Opera Reformation, Mozart brought real, flesh-and-blood human psychology onto the stage in two distinct operatic genres:

1. **Opera Buffa (Italian Comic Opera — WITH Sung Recitativo)**: Sung entirely in **Italian**, where the dialogue between arias is sung as rapid speech-like **Recitativo** (accompanied by keyboard/continuo). Instead of kings and gods, *Opera buffa* stars clever common servants (like Figaro and Susanna) who outsmart their arrogant aristocratic masters! Mozart's two greatest *opera buffa* masterpieces (both written with librettist Lorenzo Da Ponte) are:

• **«The Marriage of Figaro» (*Le nozze di Figaro*, K. 492, 1786)**: Based on a politically explosive French play by Beaumarchais (which Napoleon called *'the Revolution already in action'* because the servant Figaro outwits Count Almaviva!). On Slide 19, Prof. Chae includes the sparkling Overture and the famous Act III soprano duet **'Sull'aria'** (*'Che soave zeffiretto'* — where the Countess dictates a letter to her maid Susanna to trap her unfaithful husband), immortalized in the 1994 movie ***The Shawshank Redemption*** (where Andy Dufresne locks himself in the prison warden's office and broadcasts 'Sull'aria' over the prison PA system, and Red says: *'I have no idea to this day what those two Italian ladies were singing about... I'd like to think they were singing about something so beautiful it can't be expressed in words, and it makes your heart ache because of it.'*).

• **«Don Giovanni» (K. 527, 1787)**: Combines *opera buffa* comedy with dark supernatural tragedy (*dramma giocoso*): the unrepentant nobleman seducer Don Giovanni kills the Commendatore in a duel, and in the terrifying final scene (shown in the *Amadeus* clip on Slide 19), the stone statue of the Commendatore comes to life (voiced by booming D-minor trombones — inspired by Mozart's own father Leopold's death!) and drags Don Giovanni down to the flames of Hell!

2. **Singspiel (German 'Song-Play' — Spoken Dialogue, NO Recitativo!)**: Look closely at Prof. Chae's note on Slide 18: **`(Singspiel, no recitativo)`** and **`middle-class`**! What is a *Singspiel*? It is a popular **German-language** comic/fantasy opera written for everyday middle-class Viennese audiences in which — just like a modern Broadway musical — the characters do NOT sing recitativo between songs; instead, they speak **ordinary spoken German dialogue (NO recitativo!)** between the musical numbers! Both of Mozart's *Singspiele* are on your 49-track listening list (#37 and #38):

• **«The Abduction from the Seraglio» (*Die Entführung aus dem Serail*, K. 384, 1782 — Finale Chorus cued `from 2:10`, Listening Track #37)**: Set in a Turkish Pasha's palace; at `2:10` (after Pasha Selim forgives the Spanish lovers in spoken German dialogue), the soloists and chorus launch into a joyful Viennese-Turkish *Vaudeville* finale (*'Nie werd' ich deine Huld verkennen'*) bursting with crashing Turkish Janissary cymbals, triangle, and bass drum! (Also famous from the *Amadeus* scene where Emperor Joseph II complains there are *'too many notes'*!)

• **«The Magic Flute» (*Die Zauberflöte*, K. 620, 1791 — Queen of the Night Aria *'Der Hölle Rache'*, Listening Track #38)**: Premiered at a popular suburban theater in Vienna two months before Mozart's death. In Act II, the furious Queen of the Night hands her daughter Pamina a dagger and orders her to murder the high priest Sarastro in the most famous **coloratura soprano** aria in history: *'Der Hölle Rache kocht in meinem Herzen'* ('Hell's vengeance boils in my heart'), rocketing up to stratospheric staccato **high F6** notes! On Slide 20, Prof. Chae also plays the hilarious real-life recording of wealthy tone-deaf American socialite **Florence Foster Jenkins** (and Meryl Streep's 2016 film portrayal) attempting to screech Those high F6 notes!

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **Opera Buffa vs. Singspiel (Crucial Exam Contrast!)**: Opera Buffa = Italian comic opera with SUNG RECITATIVO (e.g., «The Marriage of Figaro», «Don Giovanni») | Singspiel = German-language opera for middle-class audiences with SPOKEN DIALOGUE and NO RECITATIVO (e.g., «The Abduction from the Seraglio» #37, «The Magic Flute» #38).
- **«The Marriage of Figaro» ('Sull'aria' / Shawshank Redemption) & «Don Giovanni»**: Mozart's two greatest Italian Opera Buffa works: 'Figaro' features the letter duet 'Sull'aria' played over the prison loudspeaker in 'The Shawshank Redemption'; 'Don Giovanni' ends with the stone statue dragging the unrepentant nobleman to Hell.
- **«The Abduction from the Seraglio» — Finale (Track #37, from 2:10)**: German Singspiel; at 2:10 the soloists and chorus sing a joyous Vaudeville finale with crashing Turkish percussion (cymbals, triangle, bass drum).

#### Slide 21 (p. 20) — Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827, Germany): Bridge to Romanticism, The 1st Viennese School & Motivic Development (Classical → Romantic Bridge (1770–1827))
- **On the Slide**: `Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827, Germany) | Classical – Romantic periods; innovation; freelancer; absolute music; structure; motive; counterpoint; improvisation | The 1st Viennese School: F. J. Haydn, W. A. Mozart, L. van Beethoven | Symphony No. 5 1st Mvt. motivic development`

Slides 21 through 28 (the final 8 slides of PPT 2) are devoted to **Ludwig van Beethoven (1770–1827)** — born in Bonn, Germany, who moved to Vienna at age 21 to study with Haydn and became the titanic bridge between the **Classical and Romantic periods**! Let's break down every keyword on Slide 21:

1. **The First Viennese School**: Music historians group **F. J. Haydn, W. A. Mozart, and L. van Beethoven** together as **'The First Viennese School'** because all three lived and worked in Vienna, Austria, and brought Classical forms (Symphony, Sonata, String Quartet, Concerto) to perfection.

2. **Bridge from Classical to Romantic (`Classical – Romantic periods; innovation`)**: Why does Beethoven belong to BOTH the Classical and Romantic eras? Because he kept the rigorous architectural **structures** of the Classical period (Sonata Form, Symphony, String Quartet, Piano Sonata, Absolute Music), but exploded their size, dynamic extremes (`ppp` to `fff`), emotional intensity, and heroic struggle — inaugurating 19th-century Romanticism! Historians divide his output into **Three Stylistic Periods**: (1) *Early Period* (to c. 1802, mastering Haydn/Mozart's Classical style, e.g., *Pathétique* Sonata #46, *Spring* Violin Sonata #45); (2) *Middle / 'Heroic' Period* (1803–1814, after confronting his progressive **deafness** in the 1802 Heiligenstadt Testament — massive heroic works like Symphony No. 3 *Eroica*, Symphony No. 5 #44, *Appassionata* Sonata #47, *Emperor* Concerto #48); and (3) *Late Period* (1815–1827, when he was completely deaf — spiritual, contrapuntal works like Symphony No. 9 *Choral* #49 and the late String Quartets).

3. **True Independent Freelancer**: If Haydn was a palace servant (Esterházy) and Mozart struggled to survive as a freelancer, Beethoven was the first composer in history treated by Viennese aristocrats as an equal — and even a superior moral genius! Three Viennese noblemen actually signed a contract paying Beethoven an annual pension with just one condition: that he continue living in Vienna and compose whatever he wished!

4. **Motivic Development (`motive; structure`)**: How does Beethoven build a massive 35-minute symphony? Instead of relying on long, flowing melodies like Mozart, Beethoven builds an entire monument out of a tiny, rhythmic **Motive** (a 4-note cell) through relentless **Motivic Development**! On Slide 21, Prof. Chae embeds the graphical score of the 1st movement of **Symphony No. 5 in C Minor, Op. 67**: the entire movement grows organically out of the famous 4-note 'Fate knocking at the door' motive (`short-short-short-LONG!`, `G–G–G–Eb`).

**Key Concepts & Terms Explained:**
- **The First Viennese School**: The three supreme Classical masters active in Vienna: 1. Franz Joseph Haydn, 2. Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart, and 3. Ludwig van Beethoven.
- **Classical–Romantic Bridge & Freelancer**: Born in Germany (1770–1827), worked in Vienna as an independent freelancer; expanded Classical structural forms (Absolute Music) with revolutionary emotional and dynamic power that launched the Romantic era.
- **Motive & Motivic Development (Symphony No. 5, 1st Mvt)**: A Motive is the smallest recognizable melodic/rhythmic cell (such as the 4-note 'short-short-short-LONG' Fate motif of Beethoven's 5th Symphony) that Beethoven repeats, sequences, and transforms to unify an entire work.

While Haydn wrote 106 symphonies and Mozart wrote 41, Beethoven composed just **9 Symphonies** — because he turned each individual symphony into a colossal, revolutionary world of its own! Across Slides 22, 23, and 24, Prof. Chae surveys his most famous symphonies and highlights two movements on your 49-track listening list (#44 Symphony No. 5 Finale and #49 Symphony No. 9 Finale):

1. **Symphony No. 3 in E-flat Major, Op. 55 ('Eroica' / 'Heroic', 1804)**: Twice as long as any symphony written before it! Beethoven originally dedicated it to **Napoleon Bonaparte** as the champion of the French Revolution's democratic ideals — but when news arrived in 1804 that Napoleon had crowned himself Emperor of France, Beethoven flew into a rage, shouted *'So he is nothing more than an ordinary tyrant!'*, and violently scratched Napoleon's name off the title page so hard he tore a hole through the paper (seen in the manuscript image), retitling it *'Sinfonia Eroica, composed to celebrate the memory of a great man'*!

2. **Symphony No. 5 in C Minor, Op. 67 (1808 — 4th Mvt *Allegro* Finale in C Major, Listening Track #44)**: Forges a psychological journey from dark struggle (**C minor** in the 1st movement) directly into blazing victory (**C Major** in the 4th movement Finale, Track #44!). To make the 4th movement as triumphant as possible, Beethoven added **three instruments never before heard in a standard Classical symphony**: **3 Trombones, a high Piccolo, and a deep Contrabassoon**! On the listening test, Track #44 opens immediately at `0:00` with that blinding C-major brass + full orchestra fanfare (`C — E — G — | F–E–D–C–D–B–C!`). 3. **Symphony No. 6 in F Major, Op. 68 ('Pastoral', 1808)**: Beethoven's rare work of **Program Music** in 5 movements depicting a day in the countryside (1. Awakening of cheerful feelings upon arriving in the country, 2. Scene by the brook, 3. Merry gathering of country folk, 4. Thunderstorm, 5. Shepherd's song of thanksgiving after the storm — animated in Disney's 1940 film ***Fantasia***).

4. **Symphony No. 7 in A Major, Op. 92 (1812)**: Richard Wagner called it the *'Apotheosis of the Dance'* for its unstoppable rhythmic drive; its solemn 2nd movement (*Allegretto*) is the climax soundtrack of the film ***The King's Speech*** (2010), and its exuberant 1st & 4th movements are the signature theme of the Japanese music drama ***Nodame Cantabile***.

5. **Symphony No. 9 in D Minor, Op. 125 ('Choral', 1824 — 4th Mvt Finale *'Ode to Joy'*, Listening Track #49)**: Composed when Beethoven was **completely deaf** (at the 1824 Vienna premiere, the contralto soloist had to gently turn Beethoven around on stage so he could see the audience standing and waving handkerchiefs in thunderous applause!). Why was Symphony No. 9 the most radical revolution in symphonic history? Because for the first time ever, Beethoven broke the boundary of purely instrumental Absolute Music by bringing **4 vocal soloists (Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass) and a full SATB Choir** into a Symphony to sing German poet **Friedrich Schiller's poem «Ode to Joy» (*An die Freude*)** proclaiming universal human brotherhood (*'Be embraced, you millions! ## Part 3: Complete 49-Track Listening Guide

### No. 1. Gregorian chant (Anonymous) — ‘Dies Irae’
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Gregorian Sequence (Roman Catholic Requiem Mass) | Male Monastic Choir, A cappella (Unaccompanied)
- **Sonic Signature**: Unaccompanied male monks singing a dark, stepwise Latin melody in unison inside a reverberant stone church with no beat and no instruments.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Gregorian chant is the foundational sacred vocal music of the Roman Catholic Church, named after Pope Gregory I (r. 590–604), who ordered the codification and standardization of liturgy across Western Europe and prohibited musical instruments in church. ‘Dies Irae’ (‘The Day of Wrath’) is a 13th-century Franciscan Latin poem (traditionally attributed to Thomas of Celano) describing the Last Judgment, when the trumpet summons souls before the throne of God. Because it was sung at every Catholic Requiem Mass (Mass for the Dead) for centuries, its opening four notes (F–E–F–D) became the universal musical symbol for death, doom, and the supernatural — quoted later by Berlioz, Liszt, Rachmaninoff, and Hollywood film composers in Star Wars (when Luke discovers his murdered aunt and uncle), The Shining, and The Lion King.
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Texture: Strictly Monophonic (every monk sings the exact same single melody line in unison with zero harmony or chords). Accompaniment: A cappella (literally ‘in the manner of the chapel’ — voices only, no instruments). Rhythm & Pitch: Non-metric free rhythm that follows the natural syllabic stress of the Latin words, written in the Dorian church mode.
- **Exam Distinction**: Only monophonic work on the entire 49-piece list. If you hear a single melody line sung by men in Latin with no chords and no instruments, it is No. 1.

### No. 2. Josquin des Prez — ‘Ave Maria … virgo serena’
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Sacred Latin Motet | 4-Voice Mixed Choir (Soprano, Alto, Tenor, Bass), A cappella
- **Sonic Signature**: Slow, angelic church a cappella singing: high female/boy soprano voices enter completely alone on a smooth rising phrase, then lower voices enter one after another imitating the exact same tune.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Josquin des Prez (c. 1440–1521) was the towering master of the Franco-Flemish High Renaissance school; Martin Luther famously remarked, ‘Other composers do what the notes require, but Josquin is master of the notes.’ Composed around 1485 while Josquin was serving in Milan, ‘Ave Maria … virgo serena’ became the most famous motet of the 16th century and was printed at the very front of Petrucci’s Motetti A (1502), the first printed collection of polyphonic motets in history. In Prof. Chae’s lecture (PPT 1, Slide 13), this piece demonstrates how Josquin perfected imitative counterpoint and canon, laying the contrapuntal foundation that directly influenced J. S. Bach in the Baroque era.
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Genre: Sacred Latin Motet (polyphonic choral work on a sacred Latin text outside the Mass Ordinary). Texture: Opens with textbook Imitative Polyphony (point of imitation): the high sopranos enter alone on a serene rising 4-note motive (‘A-ve Ma-ri-a’), followed in turn by the altos, tenors, and basses singing the exact same melody at staggered intervals. Later sections alternate duets and warm homophonic passages.
- **Exam Distinction**: Compare with No. 3 (Italian Madrigal): No. 2 is slow, sacred Latin church polyphony with high soprano voices entering first; No. 3 is faster, bouncy, conversational Italian sung by male voices only.

### No. 3. Italian Madrigal (Renaissance) — Italian Madrigal (Ensemble Amarcord)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Secular Vocal Music (16th-Century Italian Madrigal) | 5-Voice Male Vocal Ensemble (Amarcord), A cappella
- **Sonic Signature**: After 10 seconds of ambient intro, five male voices burst in with a warm, rhythmic, speech-like Italian secular song — much faster, bouncier, and more conversational than Josquin’s slow church motet (#2).
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: The Madrigal emerged in 16th-century Italy as the most important secular (non-religious) vocal genre of the Renaissance, flourishing in aristocratic Italian courts for entertainment and amateur music-making among educated noblemen and women. Unlike sacred Latin motets (#2), madrigals set expressive vernacular Italian lyric poetry about courtly love, nature, humor, and heartbreak. Madrigal composers pioneered ‘word painting’ (madrigalism) — making the music literally illustrate the text (such as rapid running notes on the word ‘chase’ or dissonant clashes on ‘pain’). In this video from PPT 1 (Slide 14), the German vocal quintet Amarcord performs a lively Renaissance madrigal.
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Genre: 16th-century Italian secular vocal piece. Texture: Alternates lively rhythmic homophony (voices pronouncing Italian syllables together in bouncy dance-like rhythms) with playful polyphonic imitation. Accompaniment: A cappella (unaccompanied voices).

### No. 4. G. Caccini — ‘Amarilli, mia bella’ (from Le nuove musiche)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Early Baroque Monody (Solo Madrigal / Aria) | Solo Soprano + Plucked Archlute (Basso Continuo)
- **Sonic Signature**: Intimate solo female soprano singing a gentle, sighing Italian melody accompanied ONLY by a plucked guitar-like archlute — zero bowed violins, zero orchestra!
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Around 1600 in Florence, Italy, a group of humanist poets, musicians, and scholars known as the Florentine Camerata (including Giulio Caccini, 1551–1618) sought to revive the emotional power of Ancient Greek drama. They argued that Renaissance polyphony (4–6 voices singing different words simultaneously) obscured the emotional meaning of poetry. To solve this, Caccini pioneered ‘Monody’ — a single expressive solo voice accompanied by simple chords improvised over a bass line (figured bass / basso continuo), which directly gave birth to Opera! Published in Caccini’s landmark 1602 collection Le nuove musiche (‘The New Music’), ‘Amarilli, mia bella’ (‘Amaryllis, my beautiful one’) is a tender pastoral love song. (Prof. Chae also notes on Slide 17 that the famous ‘Caccini’s Ave Maria’ sung by Sumi Jo is actually a 1970 musical hoax composed by Soviet lutenist Vladimir Vavilov!).
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Texture: Early Baroque Homophony / Monody (one solo vocal line supported by sparse chordal accompaniment). Accompaniment: Basso continuo (realized here on a single plucked archlute without any bowed strings or orchestra). Key: G minor / Dorian modal inflection with expressive vocal sighs and delicate Baroque trills at cadences.
- **Exam Distinction**: Easiest cue on the exam: listen to the accompaniment! No. 4 is the ONLY track on the list with a solo voice + plucked lute only (Handel’s arias No. 9 & No. 10 use male countertenors with bowed string orchestras).

### No. 5. A. Vivaldi — The Four Seasons — ‘Winter’ (L'Inverno), 1st Mvt.
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Solo Violin Concerto (Baroque Program Music) | Solo Violin + String Orchestra & Harpsichord Continuo
- **Sonic Signature**: Piercing, icy, shivering staccato notes entering layer by layer (like chattering teeth in freezing cold), suddenly interrupted by furious, lightning-fast solo violin windstorms.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Antonio Vivaldi (1678–1741), born in Venice and nicknamed ‘The Red Priest’ (Il Prete Rosso) for his red hair and priesthood, spent decades as violin master at the Ospedale della Pietà (a Venetian convent orphanage for girls famous across Europe for its virtuoso all-female orchestra). Published in 1725 as the first four concertos of Op. 8, The Four Seasons (Le quattro stagioni) are groundbreaking works of Baroque Program Music: each concerto is paired with an Italian sonnet (likely written by Vivaldi himself) printed directly in the score. In the 1st movement of ‘Winter’ (RV 297, F minor), the score explicitly labels ‘shivering with cold in the icy snow’, ‘blasts ofhorrid wind’, ‘stamping feet to stay warm’, and ‘chattering teeth’! On PPT 1 (Slide 29), Prof. Chae also highlights its appearances in Portrait of a Lady on Fire and John Wick: Chapter 3.
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Form: Baroque Ritornello Form (alternating between the recurring orchestral Tutti ritornello theme and fiery virtuosic Solo Violin episodes). Key: F minor (the only minor-key Vivaldi track on the midterm list). Technique: Rapid, biting staccato tremolos and dissonant trills over a pulsing bass.
- **Exam Distinction**: Only MINOR-KEY Vivaldi Four Seasons track on the list! Tense, icy, shivering staccato (whereas No. 6 ‘Autumn’ and No. 7 ‘Spring’ are bright, cheerful major keys).

### No. 6. A. Vivaldi — The Four Seasons — ‘Autumn’ (L'Autunno), 3rd Mvt.
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Solo Violin Concerto (Baroque Program Music) | Solo Violin + String Orchestra & Continuo
- **Sonic Signature**: Bouncy, rollicking 3/8 ‘galloping horse’ hunting rhythm (`da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM`) in a bright major key with rustic, stomping accents.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: In Vivaldi’s ‘Autumn’ Concerto in F major (RV 293), the first movement depicts peasants celebrating a bountiful harvest with wine, the second movement depicts them sleeping off their drunkenness, and this 3rd movement (Allegro) depicts ‘The Hunt’ at dawn! According to Vivaldi’s sonnet, hunters ride out at daybreak with hunting horns, muskets, and barking dogs; the terrified beast flees, is wounded amidst gunshots and barking hounds (depicted by the solo violin’s frantic runs and double-stops), and finally dies exhausted.
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Form: Ritornello Form in F Major, 3/8 compound triple meter. The orchestral ritornello imitates brass hunting horns and galloping horses with a heavy, rustic dotted rhythm (`da-da-DUM, da-da-DUM`) and open fifths, alternating with virtuosic solo violin episodes depicting the fleeing quarry.
- **Exam Distinction**: Compare with No. 7 (‘Spring’): No. 6 ‘Autumn’ 3rd Mvt has a swinging, GALLOPING 3/8 hunting-horn bounce (`da-da-DUM`), whereas No. 7 ‘Spring’ 1st Mvt is a square 4/4 march with high bird-call trills.

### No. 7. A. Vivaldi — The Four Seasons — ‘Spring’ (La Primavera), 1st Mvt.
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Solo Violin Concerto (Baroque Program Music) | Solo Violin + String Orchestra & Continuo
- **Sonic Signature**: The world-famous joyful E-major ‘Spring’ theme played LOUD (f) then immediately echoed SOFT (p), followed at 0:30 by three violins chirping high bird trills.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: ‘Spring’ in E major (RV 269) is the opening concerto of Vivaldi’s The Four Seasons (1725) and one of the most recognizable pieces of Baroque music ever written. Following Vivaldi’s sonnet line by line, the 1st movement opens with the joyous arrival of Spring, followed immediately by three solo violins chirping and trilling to welcome the season with ‘festive birdsong’, gentle murmuring streams, and a sudden dark minor-key summer thunderstorm (with rapid violin scales for lightning and low string tremolos for thunder) before the birds return!
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Form: Ritornello Form in E Major, 4/4 time. Dynamics: The opening ritornello is the textbook demonstration of Baroque Terraced Dynamics — each phrase is played first Loud (forte) and immediately repeated Soft (piano) like an echo, achieved without gradual crescendos.
- **Exam Distinction**: Instant giveaway: the iconic E-major ‘Spring’ melody with loud-then-soft terraced echoes and fluttering birdsong violin trills.

### No. 8. G. F. Handel — Oratorio Messiah — ‘For Unto Us a Child is Born’
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Sacred English Oratorio Chorus (HWV 56) | 4-Part Choir + Baroque Orchestra
- **Sonic Signature**: After a lively 15-second string intro, an ENGLISH choir sings joyful, bubbling vocal runs (‘For unto us a Child is born~’) and periodically explodes into grand homophonic shouts of ‘Wonderful! Counsellor!’
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: George Frideric Handel (1685–1759) was born in Halle, Germany (the exact same year as J. S. Bach and D. Scarlatti), trained in Italian opera in Italy, and settled permanently in London as a cosmopolitan royal composer. When London audiences grew tired of expensive Italian opera seria in the 1730s, Handel reinvented the English Oratorio — dramatic sacred works sung in English for concert halls without theatrical staging, costumes, or acting. Composed in just 24 days in 1741 and premiered in Dublin for charity, Messiah (HWV 56) sets Old and New Testament prophecies of Christ. ‘For Unto Us a Child is Born’ (from Part I, setting Isaiah 9:6) recycles a joyful Italian love duet Handel had written earlier, transforming it into a radiant nativity chorus!
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Genre: Sacred Oratorio Chorus in G Major. Texture: Masterfully alternates between light Imitative Polyphony (sopranos, tenors, altos, and basses entering one by one with bubbling 16th-note melismas on ‘is born~’) and massive, thunderous Homophonic block chords when the entire choir and orchestra shout ‘Wonderful! Counsellor! The Mighty God! The Everlasting Father! The Prince of Peace!’
- **Exam Distinction**: Only ENGLISH-language choral work on the entire 49-piece list! (Bach No. 14 is German; Mozart No. 34/35 are Latin; Mozart No. 37 and Beethoven No. 49 are German).

### No. 9. G. F. Handel — Opera Rinaldo — Aria ‘Lascia ch’io pianga’
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Baroque Opera Seria Aria (Da Capo Aria in Sarabande Rhythm) | Countertenor Soloist (Philippe Jaroussky) + Strings & Plucked Harp/Lute
- **Sonic Signature**: Delicate plucked harp/lute and warm strings in a slow 3-beat Sarabande rhythm; the high male countertenor enters very early (at 0:13) on two sighing notes (‘La-scia...’), followed by a dramatic silence.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Composed in 1711, Rinaldo (HWV 7) was the first Italian opera Handel wrote specifically for the London stage, launching his British superstardom. Set during the First Crusade in Jerusalem, the noble Christian maiden Almirena has been kidnapped by the Saracen king Argante; weeping in a palace garden, she sings ‘Lascia ch’io pianga mia cruda sorte, e che sospiri la libertà’ (‘Let me weep over my cruel fate, and sigh for liberty’). Handel originally wrote the melody in 1705 as an instrumental Asian dance (Sarabande) in his first opera Almira. On PPT 1 (Slide 28), Prof. Chae pairs this aria with the 1994 film Farinelli to explain the difference between historical Castrati (male singers castrated before puberty) and modern Countertenors (like Philippe Jaroussky here, who sings in the high soprano/alto range naturally using head voice/falsetto).
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Form: Da Capo Aria (`A–B–A'`, where the return of the A section is lavishly ornamented by the singer). Meter & Rhythm: Slow 3/2 triple meter based on the Spanish Sarabande dance (`short-LONG... pause`), giving a halting, sighing sensation of weeping.
- **Exam Distinction**: Compare with No. 10 (Handel ‘Ombra mai fu’): In No. 9, you hear plucked harp/lute right away and the singer enters at 0:13 with short, pausing 2-note sighs (‘Lascia... [pause] ch’io pianga’); in No. 10, there is a 48-second string intro and the singer holds one giant sustained note (‘Oooooombra’).

### No. 10. G. F. Handel — Opera Xerxes (Serse) — Aria ‘Ombra mai fu’ (‘Largo’)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Baroque Opera Aria (Arioso / ‘Handel’s Largo’) | Countertenor Soloist (Andreas Scholl) + String Orchestra
- **Sonic Signature**: Stately, noble 48-second string orchestra introduction; at 0:48, the countertenor enters on a long, majestic sustained single note (‘Ooooooom-bra mai fu’) before stepping smoothly upward.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Premiered in London in 1738, Xerxes (Serse, HWV 40) is a late Handel opera loosely based on the Persian King Xerxes I. Remarkably, although ‘Ombra mai fu’ (‘Never was a shade [of any plant dearer and more lovely]’) became famous in the 19th century as a solemn religious-sounding piece known as ‘Handel’s Largo’, in the actual opera it is the opening scene where King Xerxes sings an absurdly passionate love song to a backyard plane tree (sycamore tree)! The title role of King Xerxes was written for the famous Italian castrato Caffarelli, and is performed in this recording by German countertenor Andreas Scholl. Prof. Chae also notes on PPT 1 (Slide 38) that this piece was featured on the soundtrack of the Korean film Untold Scandal (스캔들 - 조선남여상열지사).

Unlike ‘Lascia ch’io pianga’ (#9), it features a long, continuous 48-second orchestral introduction in the strings before the voice enters on a long held tonic note followed by a stepping scale.
- **Exam Distinction**: Compare with No. 9 (‘Lascia ch’io pianga’): No. 10 has a lush, continuous bowed string sound (no harp plucks), a long 48s orchestral intro, and the voice holds a long sustained opening note.

### No. 11. D. Scarlatti — Harpsichord Sonata in D Minor, K. 141
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Single-Movement Baroque Keyboard Sonata | Solo Harpsichord (Jean Rondeau)
- **Sonic Signature**: Fierce, metallic solo harpsichord hammering machine-gun rapid repeated notes (`rat-a-tat-tat-tat-tat`) in D minor like a frantic Spanish guitar!
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Domenico Scarlatti (1685–1757) was born in Naples, Italy in 1685 — the exact same birth year as J. S. Bach and G. F. Handel. In 1719 he left Italy to serve as court harpsichord tutor to the gifted Portuguese Princess Maria Barbara, following her to Madrid when she became Queen of Spain. Living in Iberia for the rest of his life, Scarlatti composed 555 single-movement harpsichord sonatas (catalogued with ‘K.’ by American harpsichordist Ralph Kirkpatrick — not to be confused with Mozart’s Köchel ‘K.’!). Influenced by Spanish folk music, Sonata K. 141 in D minor imitates the fiery percussive strumming (rasgueado) and rapid repeated-note plucking of Spanish flamenco guitars!

Virtuoso Technique: Rapid-fire machine-gun repeated notes (played by rapidly alternating fingers 3-2-1 on a single key), wide leaps, and crunchy Spanish dissonances.
- **Exam Distinction**: Only solo HARPSICHORD recording on the entire 49-piece list! (Bach’s WTC #16 and Goldberg #18 are played on modern piano in the linked videos). If you hear a metallic plucked keyboard hammering rapid repeated notes, it is 100% Scarlatti K. 141.

### No. 12. J. S. Bach — Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 in D Major, BWV 1050 (1st Mvt.)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Baroque Concerto Grosso | Solo Flute, Solo Violin, Solo Harpsichord (Concertino) + String Orchestra (Ripieno)
- **Sonic Signature**: Energetic, driving D-major 16th-note string motor, immediately answered by a conversational trio of soloists: wooden Baroque Flute + Violin + glittering Harpsichord.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: In 1721, while serving as court Kapellmeister in Köthen, Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) assembled six concertos and sent them in a calligraphic dedication score to Christian Ludwig, Margrave of Brandenburg (essentially a royal job application). On PPT 1 (Slide 20), Prof. Chae uses Brandenburg Concerto No. 5 as the definitive example of the Baroque Concerto Grosso — a concerto based on competition and contrast between the full orchestra (Tutti / Ripieno) and a small group of soloists (Concertino: here a wooden Baroque flute, solo violin, and harpsichord). Historically, No. 5 is famous as the very first keyboard concerto in history: Bach had just purchased a magnificent new two-manual harpsichord from Berlin, and in the middle of the 1st movement, the harpsichord breaks out of its humble background basso continuo role to play a jaw-dropping, 65-measure unaccompanied solo cadenza!
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Genre: Concerto Grosso (BWV 1050) in D Major. Form: Ritornello Form. The brisk, driving 16th-note string ritornello alternates with airy, contrapuntal dialogues between the wooden flute, violin, and glittering harpsichord, culminating in the massive solo harpsichord cadenza.
- **Exam Distinction**: Distinguish from Vivaldi (#5–#7) and Bach Double Violin (#17) by listening for the WOODEN FLUTE and chattering SOLO HARPSICHORD racing alongside the strings.

### No. 14. J. S. Bach — Cantata BWV 147 — ‘Jesu, Joy of Man’s Desiring’

### No. 15. J. S. Bach — ‘Air on the G String’ (from Orchestral Suite No. 3 in D Major, BWV 1068)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Baroque Orchestral Suite (2nd Movement: Air) | String Orchestra + Harpsichord Continuo (No winds or brass)

- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Bach’s four Orchestral Suites (which he called Ouvertures, BWV 1066–1069) were performed in Leipzig by the Collegium Musicum — a university ensemble conducted by Bach at Zimmermann’s Coffeehouse! While the outer movements of Suite No. 3 in D Major use festive trumpets and timpani, the 2nd movement is a quiet, lyrical ‘Air’ (meaning a song-like instrumental melody, not a dance) scored for strings and continuo alone. ### No. 16. J. S. Bach — The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book 1, No. 1 in C Major, BWV 846 — Prelude
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Solo Keyboard Prelude | Solo Piano (Alexandre Tharaud)
- **Sonic Signature**: Pure, gentle, continuous upward broken-chord arpeggios in C major (`C-E-G-C-E-G-C-E`), repeating each harmony twice like calm water ripples from start to finish.
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Completed in 1722 (Book 1) and 1742 (Book 2), J. S. Bach’s The Well-Tempered Clavier (Das Wohltemperierte Klavier, BWV 846–893) consists of 48 Preludes and Fugues — two complete books of 24 pieces each, marching chromatically through every single one of the 12 major and 12 minor keys (C major, C minor, C-sharp major, C-sharp minor... all the way to B minor). Bach composed it to prove the beauty of ‘well-temperament’ (a new keyboard tuning system that allowed musicians to play in all 24 keys without retuning the instrument) and for the ‘instruction of musical youth’. On PPT 1 (Slide 37), Prof. Chae highlights that conductor Hans von Bülow famously called Bach’s 48 Well-Tempered Clavier ‘The Old Testament’ of piano music (and Beethoven’s 32 Piano Sonatas ‘The New Testament’). In 1853, French Romantic composer Charles Gounod superimposed a vocal melody directly over this exact C-major Prelude to create the famous ‘Bach/Gounod Ave Maria’ (also demonstrated on Slide 37 by Bobby McFerrin singing the prelude while the audience sings the Ave Maria melody!).

Texture: Consists entirely of uniform, unbroken 16th-note arpeggiated chords (broken chords rippling upward from the bass note through the right hand, repeating each 8-note pattern twice per measure) with zero separate melody line on top — pure harmonic progression.

### No. 17. J. S. Bach — Concerto for Two Violins in D Minor, BWV 1043 — 2nd Mvt. (Largo ma non tanto)
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Baroque Double Violin Concerto (2nd Mvt: Largo ma non tanto) | 2 Solo Violins + String Orchestra & Continuo

- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Composed during Bach’s Köthen period or for his Leipzig Collegium Musicum (c. 1730), the Concerto for Two Violins in D Minor (BWV 1043, affectionately known as the ‘Bach Double’) shows how Bach fused Italian concerto form (learned from studying Vivaldi’s scores) with dense German imitative counterpoint. ### No. 18. J. S. Bach — Goldberg Variations, BWV 988 — Aria
- **Period / Genre / Forces**: undefined | Solo Keyboard Theme & Variations (Opening Aria / Sarabande) | Solo Piano (Glenn Gould, 1981 recording)
- **Sonic Signature**: Very slow, intimate, crystalline solo piano Sarabande in G major filled with delicate Baroque trills and ornaments (you can even faintly hear Glenn Gould humming along!).
- **Historical Background & Lecture Context**: Published in 1741, the Goldberg Variations (BWV 988) consists of an opening Aria followed by 30 virtuosic variations (including canons at every interval from the unison to the ninth) and a final return of the Aria da capo. According to Bach’s first biographer Nikolaus Forkel, the work was commissioned by Count Hermann Karl von Keyserlingk, the Russian ambassador to Dresden, who suffered from severe insomnia and asked Bach for ‘some keyboard pieces of a gentle and somewhat lively character’ that his young live-in harpsichordist, Johann Gottlieb Goldberg, could play for him from an adjoining room during his sleepless nights. The linked recording is the legendary 1981 studio recording by Canadian pianist Glenn Gould. On PPT 1 (Slide 36), Prof. Chae also highlights that the Goldberg Variations Aria is the signature music of Dr. Hannibal Lecter in The Silence of the Lambs!
- **Musical Form & Texture**: Form: Binary Form (`A–B`, 16 bars + 16 bars) in G Major, 3/4 time. Style: Stylized French-style Sarabande (slow triple meter with weight on beat 2) lavishly decorated with Baroque ornaments (trills, mordents, appoggiaturas).
